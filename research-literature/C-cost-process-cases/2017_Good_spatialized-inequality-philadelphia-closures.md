# Invoking landscapes of spatialized inequality: Race, class, and place in Philadelphia's school closure debate

- **Citation:** Good, R. M. (2017). Invoking landscapes of spatialized inequality: Race, class, and place in Philadelphia's school closure debate. *Journal of Urban Affairs, 39*(3), 358-380. Related: Good, R. M. (2017). Histories that root us: Neighborhood, place, and the protest of school closures in Philadelphia. *Urban Geography, 38*(6), 861-883 (https://doi.org/10.1080/02723638.2016.1182286); Good, R. M. (2019). Neighborhood schools and community development. *Journal of Planning Education and Research* (https://doi.org/10.1177/0739456X19839769).
- **DOI / URL:** https://doi.org/10.1080/07352166.2016.1245069
- **Type:** Peer-reviewed empirical (mixed methods)
- **Peer-reviewed:** Yes
- **Subtopic:** 2. Equity, race, place and community

## Setting
Philadelphia's 2013 closures (the district recommended 37 closures and closed 23-24 schools).

## Method
Census-tract comparison of closure and non-closure tracts, plus qualitative analysis of public-meeting testimony and stakeholder interviews.

## Key findings
- Tracts with schools recommended for closure had disproportionately lower incomes and more African American residents.
- Stakeholders framed closures as (1) continuing past place-based marginalization, (2) a burden unequally borne by poor and Black communities, and (3) a harbinger of displacement and gentrification.
- The companion Urban Geography paper shows that invoking a school's history 'rooted schools in place', widening the debate beyond district metrics. The 2019 paper argues that schools are community-development assets that planners often ignore.

## Limitations / viewpoint
One city and one closure round. The quantitative part is descriptive, not causal.

**Relevance to PPS:** Suggests PPS should expect place-based and historical arguments, and should publish tract-level demographics for each closure scenario.
