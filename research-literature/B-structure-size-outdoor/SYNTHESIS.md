# Synthesis: What the Research Says About the Plan's Structural Choices

Compiled 2026-10-07. Citations link to summary files in this folder. See [`INDEX.md`](INDEX.md) for the full list.

## 1. Converting K-8 schools to K-5

**Strength: moderate to strong for achievement; moderate for behavior; thin for students with disabilities.**

The strongest studies follow the same students over time and compare them with K-8 peers. They agree on the direction:

- **New York City.** Entering a middle school lowered achievement by about 0.14-0.18 SD, and the loss persisted through grade 8. It was larger for low achievers and came with more absences ([Rockoff & Lockwood](2010_rockoff_stuck-in-the-middle.md)).
- **Florida.** Entering middle school in grade 6 cut math by 0.12 SD and reading by 0.09 SD. The losses grew, did not recover in high school, and early dropout rose by 1.4 points ([Schwerdt & West](2013_schwerdt_grade-configurations-florida.md)).
- **Elsewhere.** British Columbia ([Dhuey](2013_dhuey_middle-school-bc.md)) and a national U.S. sample ([Kieffer](2013_kieffer_k8-ecls.md); reading only) agree.
- **High-school completion.** Switching to grade 6-8 middle schools reduced on-time completion ([Bedard & Do](2005_bedard_middle-schools-hs-completion.md)).
- **Discipline.** Middle-school sixth graders had more infractions, and the gap persisted to grade 9 ([Cook et al.](2008_cook_sixth-grade-middle-school-nc.md); covariate-adjusted, not a natural experiment).

Likely mechanisms are larger schools and grade cohorts, weaker classroom quality and lost continuity ([Holas & Huston](2012_holas_are-middle-schools-harmful.md); [Byrnes & Ruby](2007_byrnes_k8-vs-middle-philadelphia.md); [Alspaugh](1998_alspaugh_transition-achievement-loss.md); [Eccles et al.](1993_eccles_stage-environment-fit.md); [Evans et al.](2018_evans_transition-secondary-review.md)).

**Conflicts:**

- [Dove et al.](2010_dove_grade-span-arkansas.md) found no difference, but with a weak school-level design.
- [Weiss & Kipnes](2006_weiss_k8-vs-middle-philadelphia.md) found few grade-8 differences.
- Newly converted K-8s showed no advantage ([Byrnes & Ruby](2007_byrnes_k8-vs-middle-philadelphia.md)).

No meta-analysis pools these effects. None of these studies reports results for students with disabilities, so any claim about them rests on indirect evidence.

**Implication.** The conversions carry a real but not certain risk of achievement, attendance and behavior costs, concentrated among lower achievers. The burden of proof sits with the plan.

## 2. School size and minimum thresholds

**Strength: moderate; mostly correlational, with a few strong causal studies.**

[Leithwood & Jantzi](2009_leithwood_school-size-review.md) (57 studies) favor smaller schools, especially for disadvantaged students. They suggest *maximums* of about 300 (high-poverty elementary), 500 (other elementary), 600 (high-poverty secondary) and 1,000 (other secondary). Other studies support this:

- 600-900 was the most effective high-school size ([Lee & Smith](1997_lee_high-school-size.md)).
- The local NWREL synthesis agrees ([Cotton](1996_cotton_school-size-climate.md); not peer-reviewed).
- Smaller schools causally raised math and attendance ([Kuziemko](2006_kuziemko_school-size-shocks.md)).
- Small high schools of choice raised graduation by 9.5 points, though smallness was bundled with other reforms ([Bloom & Unterman](2014_bloom_small-high-schools-nyc.md)).

**Counterweight.** In Denmark, where schools are small, larger schools had small positive long-run effects ([Humlum & Smith](2015_humlum_long-term-school-size.md)). Cost economies appear mainly at the very small end ([Andrews et al.](2002_andrews_economies-of-size.md); numeric ranges unverified).

**Consolidation.** Students in Danish schools that closed lost achievement in the short run, most of all those from small schools ([Beuchert et al.](2018_beuchert_school-consolidation-denmark.md)). Larger grade cohorts harmed disadvantaged students and students with learning disabilities, with no average effect ([Gershenson & Langbein](2015_gershenson_primary-school-size.md)).

**Implication.** No study found here sets a research-based *minimum* size. PPS's floors (360 for K-5, 500 for K-8 or middle, 1,100 for high school) sit at or above the reviews' suggested maximums for disadvantaged students. They appear to rest on cost and program arguments, not achievement evidence.

## 3. Class size in receiving schools

**Strength: strong but contested.**

- **Tennessee STAR.** Large reductions in grades K-3 raised scores by about 4-5 percentiles, more for low-income and minority students ([Krueger](1999_krueger_star.md)), and raised college attendance ([Chetty et al.](2011_chetty_star-long-term.md)).
- **Nulls.** Natural-variation studies find null effects ([Hoxby](2000_hoxby_class-size-population-variation.md)). The Campbell meta-analysis finds at best small reading effects ([Filges et al.](2018_filges_class-size-campbell.md)).
- **Advocacy.** [Schanzenbach](2014_schanzenbach_class-size-nepc.md) (not peer-reviewed) argues increases harm outcomes.

**Implication.** Modest increases probably cost little on average. The risk is greatest in early grades and high-poverty classrooms.

## 4. Outdoor and environmental education (Sunnyside)

**Strength: low to moderate. Achievement effects are mixed and low quality; engagement and well-being effects are more consistent.**

The systematic reviews are cautious:

- Only 13 studies of regular outdoor teaching exist, and the quantitative ones are low quality ([Becker et al.](2017_becker_outdoor-regular-classes.md)).
- Across 147 studies, social, engagement and well-being benefits are consistent but academic results "mixed" ([Mann et al.](2022_mann_nature-outdoor-learning-review.md)).
- There is little evidence of broad academic gains for ages 10-18 ([Lorimer et al.](2026_lorimer_nature-programmes-secondary-scoping.md)).
- For school greenness, 64% of findings were null ([Browning & Rigolon](2019_browning_school-green-space-review.md); see also [Díaz-Martínez et al.](2023_diaz-martinez_school-green-blue-neurodevelopment.md)).
- Garden and environmental-education syntheses are mostly positive but built on weak designs ([Williams & Dixon](2013_williams_garden-based-learning.md); [Ardoin et al.](2018_ardoin_ee-k12-outcomes.md)).

**Stronger single studies:**

- The one large randomized trial found "very modest" science gains ([Wells et al.](2015_wells_school-gardens-rct.md)).
- A Danish controlled study found small reading gains ([Otte et al.](2019_otte_eotc-reading.md)).
- [Kuo, Barnes & Jordan](2019_kuo_nature-promotes-learning.md) argue more confidently, in a narrative review by advocates.
- The SEER report ([Lieberman & Hoody](1998_lieberman_seer-eic.md)) is **not peer-reviewed** and calls itself "not a quantitative assessment".

**Students with disabilities:**

- Children with ADHD concentrated better after time in nature (d = 0.52-0.77; [Faber Taylor & Kuo](2009_taylor_adhd-walk-in-park.md); n = 17).
- Observational links between nature and lower ADHD symptoms are consistent ([Hood & Baumann](2024_hood_nature-adhd-review.md)).
- Outdoor lessons improved later classroom engagement ([Kuo et al. 2018](2018_kuo_lessons-in-nature-engagement.md); two classrooms).
- For students with emotional, cognitive or behavioral disabilities, teachers reported better attention and behavior *outdoors*, with no academic cost and no indoor spillover ([Szczytko et al.](2018_szczytko_outdoor-ee-disabilities.md)).
- Autism benefits come mostly from equine therapy, not schooling ([Fan et al.](2023_fan_nature-autism-meta.md)).

**Implication.** The evidence supports saying Sunnyside's model likely benefits engagement, behavior and well-being, plausibly more for students with disabilities. It does not support claims of proven test-score gains.

## 5. Focus-option and magnet schools

**Strength: moderate (lottery-based) but heterogeneous.**

- Lotteries show achievement gains in some settings ([Bifulco et al.](2009_bifulco_interdistrict-magnets-connecticut.md)) and college gains in others ([Deming et al.](2014_deming_charlotte-choice-lottery.md)).
- Elsewhere the benefits are mainly behavioral ([Cullen et al.](2006_cullen_chicago-choice-lotteries.md); [Engberg et al.](2014_engberg_magnet-lotteries-attrition.md)). Engberg et al. also find that lottery losers often leave the district.
- Magnet choice can increase race and class sorting in the neighborhood schools left behind ([Saporito](2003_saporito_magnet-choice-segregation.md)).

**Implication.** The equity argument against focus options has general support. Whether it applies to Sunnyside depends on its actual draw and demographics.
