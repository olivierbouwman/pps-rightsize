# Growing Pains: The School Consolidation Movement and Student Outcomes

**Citation:** Berry, C. R., & West, M. R. (2010). Growing pains: The school consolidation movement and student outcomes. *Journal of Law, Economics, and Organization, 26*(1), 1-29. (Online first 2008.)

**DOI / URL:** https://doi.org/10.1093/jleo/ewn015

**Type:** Quasi-experimental (cross-state timing variation)

**Peer-reviewed:** Y

**Setting:** United States, 1930-1970 consolidation movement.

**Sample:** Adults in the 1980 US Census Public-Use Micro-Sample, linked to the school and district size in their state of birth during schooling.

**Method:** Exploits variation across states in the timing of consolidation to estimate effects of school and district size on attainment and earnings.

## Key findings

- Between 1930 and 1970, average US school size rose from 87 to 440 students; more than 120,000 schools and 100,000 districts were eliminated.
- Students educated in states with smaller schools had higher returns to education and completed more years of schooling.
- Students from states with larger schools earned significantly lower wages later in life.
- Larger districts were associated with modestly better outcomes, but these gains were far outweighed by the harm of larger schools.

## Limitations

- Historical setting with schools far smaller than today's; state-level variation is coarse and other state policies may confound.

## Relevance to PPS

Long-run evidence that bigger schools (as opposed to bigger districts) can carry costs. PPS consolidations increase school size; the historical baseline was much smaller, so magnitudes do not transfer directly.
