# Consolidation of Schools and Districts: What the Research Says and What It Means

**Citation:** Howley, C., Johnson, J., & Petrie, J. (2011). *Consolidation of schools and districts: What the research says and what it means*. Boulder, CO: National Education Policy Center.

**DOI / URL:** https://nepc.colorado.edu/publication/consolidation-schools-districts (ERIC ED515900)

**Type:** Policy review (narrative synthesis)

**Peer-reviewed:** N (NEPC brief, editorial-board blind review; not a journal)

**Setting:** United States, mainly rural school and district consolidation.

**Sample:** Research literature on economies of scale, school/district size and consolidation outcomes.

**Method:** Narrative synthesis.

## Key findings

- A century of consolidation has already captured most available efficiencies; in the largest jurisdictions consolidation may have produced diseconomies of scale.
- Contemporary research does not support claims of widespread fiscal or educational benefits from further consolidation; larger districts tend to add mid-level administrators.
- Impoverished regions in particular often benefit from smaller schools and districts and can suffer lasting damage from consolidation.
- Recommends case-by-case decisions, avoiding statewide mandates and arbitrary minimum sizes, and considering alternatives such as shared services.

## Limitations

- Authors are long-standing small-schools researchers; review is not systematic.
- Focus is mainly rural; less direct evidence on urban elementary consolidation.

## Relevance to PPS

Cautions against assuming savings and achievement gains from consolidation; relevant to the PPS argument that larger consolidated elementaries will be more efficient and better resourced.
