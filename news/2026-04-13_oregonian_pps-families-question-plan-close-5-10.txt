For the seven members of the Portland school board, the plan to close between five and 10 schools districtwide in the next 18 months is virtually a foregone conclusion.
The hundreds of parents who showed up Saturday afternoon to the district’s kickoff engagement session on downsizing its system were not so certain closures are inevitable.
The goal of Saturday’s session at Kellogg Middle School in Southeast Portland was for school board members and district leaders alike to hear community perspectives on what factors they should weigh as they settle on which schools should shutter amid ballooning budgets and cratering enrollment, which has been fueled by declining birthrates and high housing costs.
“Is it distance to school? Is it building conditions? What are the things that keep you up at night so we can take them into account while making our decisions?” board member Virginia La Forte asked of the crowd.
Many of the parents in the room were from the 20-plus elementary schools throughout the city that currently enroll fewer than 300 students.
“We have a strong neighborhood school community, with personal relationships between students and teachers,” said Kether Hayden, a parent at Jason Lee K-5 in Northeast Portland. “There’s a lot of fear around it being closed because of dropping enrollment.”
Assistant Superintendent Margaret Calvert took pains to emphasize that just because a school has low enrollment and is under-utilized does not mean that it will close. Instead, board members must weigh a complicated stew of factors, including the physical condition of each building, how close it is to other schools given transportation costs and safe pedestrian routes to school and the demographics of students and families being asked to uproot from their neighborhood schools. They are expected to vote on which schools would close by next November; building closures and mergers could begin in fall 2027.
Still, several parents said they were not sure how district leaders had already arrived at the conclusion that neighborhood schools needed to close and suggested that the district should first close the magnet schools scattered throughout the city that pull some families out of their neighborhood schools in favor of programming focused on arts, science or languages.
“Why are we all accepting that school closures are inevitable?” asked Arleta parent and PTA secretary Brian Dombeck, who also has a child in a pre-K program at Marysville Elementary School. “I want them to show their work before we get to that. I want to see how the costs to learners are being factored in.”
A large contingent of families from Creston Elementary, which has 213 students and is only using 60% of available building space, said their principal’s tireless efforts to visit local preschools and tell their school’s story had resulted in a bump in enrollment in kindergarten this year, providing a blueprint for raising enrollment organically. Parents are trying to help too, they said — yard signs have sprouted up around the neighborhood and there’s a large parent-led walking group.
Such small schools are typically tight-knit, supportive environments. Former Jason Lee student Luci Geny, who now attends the 572-student Roseway Heights Middle School, teared up while talking to school board chair Eddie Wang about the impact of neighborhood schools.
“Being in a more inclusive community with different people from different backgrounds made me a more compassionate and better person,” Geny said.
But having so many very small schools also puts economies of scale out of reach for Portland Public Schools. With budgets tight, district leaders say it’s difficult to afford two classrooms at each grade level, meaning that class sizes may paradoxically be larger at very small schools; it’s also harder to offer full-time arts teachers, librarians and reading specialists, among others.
Some under-enrolled schools were built to house twice as many students as currently attend; for example, Arleta K-5 in the Mount Scott neighborhood enrolls just 230 students, and only 35% of its space is currently being used, according to the district’s figures. In North Portland, just 160 students attend Rosa Parks K-5, and only 25% of the building is in use. By contrast, at Ainsworth K-5 in Southwest Portland, there are 560 students and 92% of the building is in use.
It is not entirely clear how much money closing schools will save. Michelle Morrison, the district’s chief financial officer, has said elementary school operating budgets are roughly $4 million and consolidating schools means reducing at least some of the associated personnel and utilities costs. But the school district would still need to maintain or find tenants for any buildings it decides to close.
Two other engagement sessions are scheduled next week to hear from Portland Public Schools families on school closures and consolidations: A 6 p.m. virtual-only session Monday evening and an in-person forum at Wells High School in Southwest Portland on Wednesday, April 22, at 6 p.m.
