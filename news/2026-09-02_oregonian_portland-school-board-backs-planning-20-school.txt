Title: Portland school board backs planning for up to 20 school closures
Outlet: The Oregonian/OregonLive
Author: Julia Silverman
Date: 2026-09-02

Five of the seven Portland Public Schools board members directed the district Tuesday night to continue working on scenarios that could close up to 20 schools.
Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong had asked the board to weigh in as the district’s school closure and consolidation plans move into a critical stage over the next three months. If the board’s preference was to scale back the scope of the proposed changes, Armstrong said district staff needed to know, so they could adjust their work accordingly.
The district has contended that the number of school buildings it has is no longer sustainable, given rising operational costs and rapidly declining enrollment, caused by high housing prices, fewer families moving to Portland, a lower birth rate and some families choosing private, religious, virtual or home school options for their children.
“We have fewer students today spread across a system designed to serve significantly more in too many places,” Armstrong said. “That means we are stretching our people, programs and resources too thinly to consistently provide the educational experience we want for every student.”
Closing up to 15 elementary schools will allow the district to have at least 360 students in all the remaining K-5 schools by the 2031-32 school year, said Margaret Calvert, executive director for school planning and modernization. That’s a key threshold, at which PPS could provide multiple homerooms at each grade level, principals, arts, gym, counselors and librarians that don’t need to split their time between several different schools and broad support for after-school programming, she said.
The middle school picture is less clear. The district is aiming for at least 500 students per middle school, but would most likely need to phase out some K-8 schools to reach that threshold. Closing three middle schools would put eight schools above that benchmark, but still leave four below it, Calvert said.
Five board members — Chair Eddie Wang, Vice Chair Rashelle Chase-Miller and Directors Virginia La Forte, Michelle DePass and Patte Sullivan — said they were willing to let district staff continue to model plans that would close up to 20 schools, though both Sullivan and Chase-Miller stressed that the district still had a host of questions to answer over the next few months.
Among them: What will the cost savings be? Will closures and consolidations be accompanied by layoffs, and if so, at what scale? What kind of school experience can teachers, staff and families expect at the remaining schools? What happens to the buildings that will be closed, and how much will it cost to maintain them?
Others were more definitive.
DePass said she prefers a rip the Band-Aid off approach rather than a piecemeal one, and then prioritizing extra support for schools and communities as they adjust to changes. Otherwise, she suggested, the district could find itself having to painfully revisit the closure conversation again and again in the coming years.
Two board members, Stephanie Engelsman and Christy Splitt, said they were less comfortable with the magnitude of change being proposed. Engelsman said she’d prefer that the district focus first on redrawing attendance boundaries in an attempt to smooth out enrollment districtwide first. She also said she needed more information, including about whether families in the past have left the district when schools have closed. Splitt said she was troubled by the rise in the number of school buildings that might have to close from initial numbers of about 5 to10 floated last spring.
The meeting was also punctuated with testimony from parents making the case for their children’s schools to be spared and from the Portland Association of Teachers, whose president, Alisha Chavez, criticized the closure process.
“You cannot close schools without increasing the pressure on the schools that remain,” Chavez said. “Students that move get absorbed into different places and the educators that remain, that weren’t laid off in the process, are expected to somehow make it work.”
Parent Matt Coolidge, who has a first grader at Rieke Elementary in Southwest Portland, said he was dismayed by the district’s strategy.
“Instead of simply focusing on closing schools, I would love to hear the school board’s view on how we can better recruit more of the many, many unenrolled students in the district into PPS, for a more sustainable future,” he said.
