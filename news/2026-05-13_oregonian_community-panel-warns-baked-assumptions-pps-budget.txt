Title: Community panel warns that baked-in assumptions in PPS budget may worsen its deficit

Portland Public Schools’ annual budget is convoluted, opaque and difficult for the layperson to understand, according to the 17 community members charged with providing a broader perspective on it to school board members.
The pointed review from the members of the Community Budget Review Committee — which also called for significantly more transparency around spending on third party contractors and outside vendors — arrives with just about six weeks until the school board votes to approve the $1.09 billion operating budget for the 2026-2027 school year.
Committee members include parents, students and school district staff, including outgoing Portland Association of Teachers President Angela Bonilla, who is the committee’s vice chair. The group’s feedback is intended to inform board members as they weigh final choices around the budget amid an outpouring of eleventh-hour lobbying attempts to protect school-level programs and staff from getting cut.
“We know that families and staff of the district are the most important advocates in school funding,” committee member Anne Cherry, an Arleta Elementary School parent, told board members Tuesday night. “But they can only advocate effectively, and they are only going to be motivated to do so when they can understand where the money goes and how it is being spent.”
Among the concerns in the report that committee members presented to school board members this week were that the 2026-2027 budget assumes that the district’s labor unions will agree to accept only a 1% cost of living adjustment.
That is far below the 8% increase that the Portland Association of Teachers is seeking for next year, and less than historical COLAs, which more typically range between 3-6%; the report characterized it as “unreasonably optimistic.”
If the COLA that the district eventually agrees upon with its unions is even a percentage point or two higher, that would mean even deeper budget deficits next year and in years to come, the report warns.
The budget writers may also have been overly rosy in their projections of local property tax revenue, given ongoing vacancy rates in downtown Portland, the report notes, and may have left the district open to another mid-year budget correction, which this year resulted in three days lopped off the school calendar and staff losing a paid holiday.
Other key highlights and areas of concern include:
- The two-week timeframe for thoughtful review of the massive budget — which tops $2 billion once voter-backed bond spending on modernization and construction efforts is factored in — is too compressed to yield meaningful review and recommendations, particularly in a year when the district had to make both midyear budget reductions and is cutting $56 million from operations in the coming school year.
- It is difficult to isolate how much money Portland Public Schools is spending on capital construction and debt service payments versus how much it spends on day-to-day operations. That in turn makes it hard to pinpoint per-pupil spending and how the $104 million raised by a local option levy to support teachers and programming is being spent, the report asserts.
- A lack of clarity around the impact on student learning from blended classrooms at the K-5 level, one of the district’s key money-saving strategies as enrollment has declined in elementary schools. Blended classrooms include students from multiple grades.
A big leap in the “purchased services” category, which includes third party contractors that provide services like auditing, legal assistance and moving costs. Spending in that category grew more than 60% from the current school year, to $437 million for next year. Some, but not all of that increase is due to the decision to spend $61.5 million in bond funding to hire a private firm to manage multiple massive construction projects over the next four years.
