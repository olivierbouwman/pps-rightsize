Title: Portland students could see shortened school year as budget gap grows to $14M
Outlet: The Oregonian/OregonLive
Author: Julia Silverman
Date: 2026-03-07

The Portland Association of Teachers says its members have been asked to take up to four furlough days before the end of the current school year to patch a $14 million midyear budget deficit.
That is $4 million more than school district leaders had acknowledged facing in late February, when the unexpected budget deficit first came to light.
“PPS has explicitly said that all admin will take more furlough days than PAT members,” PAT President Angela Bonilla wrote in an update to union members that was shared with The Oregonian/OregonLive. “Bargaining is a two-way street, and we will start off with a strong demand and keep in mind that our priority is to avoid the immediate layoff of over 200 PAT members.”
She added that the union was disinclined to “bail out” district leaders without “major concessions on administrator and district power and educator autonomy.”
Which days could get axed from the calendar, along with whether there will still need to be employee layoffs after spring break, is still an open question. Both sides have said negotiations are continuing. Sydney Kelly, a spokesperson for the school district, said Friday that leaders planned to have more concrete details to share early next week.
But school district sources confirmed to The Oregonian/OregonLive that the request for four days without pay had been made to the teachers union. With the school year rapidly winding down, there are only two full days left on the calendar when educators are paid but students are not scheduled to be in school: A grading day on April 3 and a planning day on April 6.
Teachers are typically reluctant to agree to give up that time, which means that depending on negotiations, students and families could wind up with four fewer days in school, most likely cut from the end of the school year in June. Potentially, school would end on Wednesday, June 3, instead of Tuesday, June 9, as scheduled. Alternatively, both sides could agree to layoffs in lieu of furloughs; about 85% of the district’s budget is spent on personnel.
In her update to teachers, Bonilla said bargaining team members were concerned about a myriad of scheduling impacts. She called for lawmakers, who just wrapped up this year’s legislative session, to reconvene for an emergency session to tap into the state’s $1 billion, lottery-fed Education Stability Fund, and for Gov. Tina Kotek to declare a state of emergency in order to do so.
Kotek previously told reporters at an end-of-session press conference that she did not “anticipate additional dollars outside the current budget for our schools and universities.”
The $14 million gap in the current academic year’s budget is separate from the $50 million that the school district is seeking to cut from its operating budget for the upcoming 2026-2027 school year.
Michelle Morrison, Chief Financial Officer for Portland Public Schools, has said problems with the current budget are due to the following factors:
- Less-than-expected savings from jobs left vacant after resignations and retirements that have unfolded during the course of the school year.
- Costs of responding to multiple federal lawsuits, including a case filed by a high school math teacher alleging that the district allowed his colleagues to promote and distribute “anti-Israel material,” a Washington, D.C. think tank’s suit alleging that the formula the district uses to allocate staff is biased in favor of schools with higher proportions of non-white students, and an investigation by the Trump administration into the district’s pending Center for Black Student Excellence. The district is also facing an investigation from the U.S. Department of Education’s Office of Civil Rights over its policies on transgender athletes.
- Services that were slated for cuts being restored without equivalent trims made elsewhere. Examples include positions added to schools to help meet student needs, Kelly said, as well as increased costs in transportation and maintenance.
- Emergency facility spending, including a steam leak at Cleveland High School, a cooling system and guard rail repairs at Benson High School, a broken pipe at Sabin Elementary, a sewer break at Rigler Elementary and other significant repairs at Sitton and Buckman elementary schools.
- Revenues from the district’s local operating levy are $5 million less than predicted because of lower commercial property tax collections, an issue that is also dogging the city of Portland’s budget.
