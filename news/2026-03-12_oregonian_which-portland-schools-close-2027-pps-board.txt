Title: Which Portland schools could close in 2027? PPS board launches districtwide review

Portland school board members unanimously agreed this week to launch a nine-month review to identify underenrolled schools within the district that should be closed.
Exactly how many and which schools will close, and how that will overlap with the school district’s parallel efforts to spend $100 million shoring up some of its oldest brick buildings against earthquakes is still unknown. Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong said Tuesday that as many as 10 schools could make the closure list she’ll present to board members next fall.
The unity among board members is notable, particularly given that the school district’s two most recent efforts at elementary and middle school consolidations and attendance zone updates were widely considered scattershot and disjointed.
“We can say right-sizing, we can say optimal use of schools, or growing great schools, but in the end I want to call it what it is, which will be a recommendation to close a subset of schools,” Armstrong told board members at a Tuesday night meeting.
If all goes as planned, board members will be voting on closures just months before school board elections in May 2027, in which Chair Eddie Wang, Vice Chair Michelle DePass and board member Patte Sullivan would all be up for reelection. Sullivan will not run for reelection, while Wang and DePass both said they are undecided, potentially making it easier to take a politically tough vote.
Enrollment at Portland Public Schools has dropped 12% since the pandemic, and is forecast to drop another 12% over the next decade, a decline of about 5,100 students or three high schools worth. That’s courtesy of a combination of declining birthrates, rising home prices that have made it difficult for young families to put down roots in the city and parents seeking different educational options for their children.
So far, the drop has been most evident in the city’s 45 elementary schools, the smallest of which, Rosa Park K-5 in North Portland, has just 160 students this year. The largest of the city’s elementary schools, Ainsworth K-5 in Southwest Portland, has 560 students.
Median enrollment at K-5 schools within Portland Public Schools has slipped to just 269 students, far lower than most suburban elementary schools in the metro area, which typically enroll between 400 and 700 students.
That enrollment gulf makes it financially difficult to offer equitable programming, said Assistant Superintendent Margaret Calvert, since larger schools can support more homerooms at each grade level and more full-time art, music and physical education classes.
Throughout the spring, summer and early fall, school board members are expected to weigh various scenarios on closures and redrawn attendance zones, taking the physical condition of buildings and their geographic locations into account. They will try to prevent the upheaval from falling disproportionately on poorer areas where communities may have less political pull and space to mobilize and advocate, said Wang, the school board chair.
To avoid such outcomes, which have been a hallmark of past efforts, the school district has contracted with a consulting firm run by a professor at the University of Texas at Austin, which will analyze what can be done differently this time.
Wang said the district needed to seek outside advice, even amid the current cutbacks that will see $50 million chopped from next year’s budget, because previous years of cuts have left the central office without capacity for the job.
“Right-sizing is not something we do every day, or even every year,” he said. “It’s expensive to keep on staff who are experts in right-sizing.”
Closing schools and redrawing attendance zones is typically an emotionally fraught conversation. Recent efforts to end high school programming at the alternative Metropolitan Learning Center in Northwest Portland, which currently enrolls about 40 students in grades 9-12, caused a community uproar and resulted in the school district reversing the decision.
On Tuesday, board members made it clear that there should be no protected schools in the review, even magnet schools that are highly popular with families who have the time and means to transport their children away from their neighborhood schools.
“Everything should be on the table in terms of how we are evaluating the big picture and how we are resourcing communities,” school board member Rashelle Chase-Miller said. “I don’t think kids should have to go to a special school, or a school out of their neighborhood, to have an amazing experience.”
Her colleague, board member Virginia La Forte, said that the 5 to 10 schools floated by Armstrong could be too low a number, particularly given the narrower band of electives available to middle schoolers who attend K-8 schools, which she suggested should be phased out.
“Consolidating at scale would allow more significant upgrades to a smaller number of schools so we have as many students as possible in the best learning environments we can provide,” La Forte said.
The conversation is kicking off just as school board members are reviewing a list of schools chosen to receive a share of $100 million in funds earmarked for seismic retrofits from the 2025 voter-backed bond. The two conversations are intertwined because schools that receive millions of dollars worth of investments to shore up their buildings in case of an earthquake are unlikely to be future closure candidates.
As a result, schools that did not enroll at least 300 students were not prioritized for the current round of funding, “given the right-sizing process that we will be engaging in,” Senior Chief of Operations Jon Franco wrote in a memo to the school board.
As it stands, the $100 million is nowhere near enough to address seismic needs districtwide; the consulting group hired to assess the district’s facilities has said doing so would carry a price tag of about $900 million.
Instead, after months of back-and-forth over how to balance how likely a school is to crumble in an earthquake with socioeconomic and demographic factors, district officials have settled on a formula that assigns 90% of its weight to seismic risk.
A group of parents led by a contingent from Cesar Chavez K-8 in North Portland has pushed back on that formula. In a letter sent to school board members earlier this week, Cesar Chavez parent Lindsay Jensen criticized “the absence of equity in the selection process,” while an open letter signed by dozens of parents at the school contends that the district had not been transparent about why some schools made the list and others did not. They’ve called for more community engagement before final decisions are made and for equity concerns to have greater weight in the formula.
At Tuesday night’s meeting, DePass said she agreed with the call for more community feedback on equity concerns, but Wang said the question was fundamentally “an engineering problem” and that urgency was critical to maximize the money available.
Those chosen to receive a portion of the $100 million from the 2025 bond include Beverly Cleary K-8 and Rose City Park K-5, both in Northeast Portland, which are slated for retrofitting work and will need to move their students to a yet-to-be-determined swing site while that work is underway.
The $100 million is also expected to pay for smaller projects concentrated in portions of buildings where students congregate at Ainsworth and Capitol Hill K-5s in Southwest Portland, Kelly and Richmond elementaries in Southeast Portland and Beach K-5 in North Portland. Additional upcoming seismic work at Vernon and Winterhaven K-8s will be paid for by a previous bond, approved by voters in 2020.
Franco said Tuesday night that Beverly Cleary K-8 and Rose City Park K-5 are not going to be candidates for closure, though they could potentially absorb students from other schools once their complete seismic retrofits are finished. But plans for the other buildings receiving partial upgrades could shift depending on decisions about right-sizing, given that the work is not scheduled to begin until summer 2027, he said.
The current plans leave out a handful of schools that are at or near the top of the seismic risk spectrum, including Glencoe and Grout elementaries in Southeast Portland, Jackson Middle School in Southwest Portland and Roseway Heights Middle School in Northeast Portland.
That doesn’t automatically mean that those schools will be on the list of future closures, Wang said. Instead, per Franco’s memo to board members, “more detailed investigation and long-range facilities planning” needs to occur, but such schools remain “high priorities for future funding.”
