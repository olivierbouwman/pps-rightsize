PORTLAND, Ore. — Portland Public Schools leaders will take the first step Tuesday evening in talking about plans to potentially close 14 schools, cuts that the district says are needed to close a massive budget deficit caused by years of declining enrollment.
So far, there are three scenarios in mind: closing schools, consolidating schools, or doing nothing — for now.
Three scenarios for the future of PPS
Under Scenario A, 14 schools would close — 13 elementary schools and one middle school. The schools include:
- Maplewood Elementary School
- Stephenson Elementary School
- Irvington Elementary School
- Rose City Park Elementary
- Buckman Elementary School
- Creston Elementary School
- Marysville Elementary School
- Woodmere Elementary School
- Lewis Elementary School
- Beach Elementary School
- James John Elementary School
- Peninsula Elementary School
- Sabin Elementary School
- Sellwood Middle School
Scenario B would close 11 schools. Stephenson, Rose City Park and Lewis elementary schools would remain open under that proposal.
A third option would defer the decision and keep the district’s current configuration for now.
School board member Virginia La Forte said she is concerned about what closures could mean for neighborhoods across Portland.
“I want to make sure we don't have areas of the city that we are leaving as essentially school deserts without any center of the community because schools are the heartbeat of our communities,” La Forte said.
La Forte is not approaching the decision only as a school board member. One of her children is still enrolled in Portland Public Schools, while another is now in college after attending PPS.
“I am just as anxious as every other parent in Portland right now,” La Forte said. “I am taking it in too. I am lying awake at night, too.”
She called the prospect of losing neighborhood schools “devastating.”
“It's devastating to communities that lose their schools,” La Forte said.
Changes could reach far beyond schools that close
Even families whose schools remain open could see significant changes.
The proposals include consolidating elementary and middle schools, changing attendance boundaries, and moving or combining programs. Dual-language immersion programs could be relocated or combined at designated schools.

“The last thing I would do would be to close community schools,” he said.
Francis said he would rather pay another tax than see his children's schools shut down. While closing schools could help address the district's financial problems, he worries the disruption could accelerate another problem facing PPS: declining enrollment.
“If you can't even rely on where your kid's going to go year to year, you're going to look for any option but PPS,” Francis said.
Teachers union calls on district to slow down
The Portland Association of Teachers is pushing back on the district’s timeline, arguing PPS should take more time before making changes that could affect more than two dozen schools and programs.
“Decisions of that magnitude take time and care,” the union said in a statement, calling for a “more measured and community centered approach.”
The union said closing, consolidating or changing programs at more than 25 schools represents a dramatic shift and called on the district to involve more students, families and educators before moving forward.
“Closing schools is not a plan; it's a reaction,” the union said. “Our students deserve better. Families deserve better. Educators deserve better.”
The union also called on PPS to exhaust other options before closing schools and said it would continue advocating for additional education funding.
‘We are going to have to take some action’
La Forte said board members have to balance the concerns of the communities they represent against the financial realities facing the district.
Nothing has been decided, she said, and the proposals could change over the next two months, but keeping every school open may not ultimately be financially possible.
“It does seem unrealistic,” La Forte said. “I think we are going to have to take some action. Definitely. What that will look like? That's what the next two months are for.”
Any changes approved by the school board would begin taking effect during the 2027-28 school year, with full implementation taking three years.
