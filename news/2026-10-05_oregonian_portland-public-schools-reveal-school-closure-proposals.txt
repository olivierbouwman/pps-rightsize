Staff at Portland Public Schools will go public with their long-awaited scenarios for which combination of K-5, K-8 and middle schools they are recommending for closures, attendance boundary adjustments and program shifts on Monday evening, ahead of what promises to be an emotional, standing room only school board meeting Tuesday night.
Parents around the city are bracing themselves for the news, which will reshape the lives of thousands of children. Principals around the district are hosting meetings at the end of the school day to discuss the proposals with their teachers and support staff.
The closures come as the school district grapples with declining enrollment and rising operational costs. Portland is far from alone: School districts around the country are coping with similar demographic shifts, thanks primarily to falling birth rates, along with a modest rise in the number of families seeking out private, religious or online schools, or choosing to homeschool their children.
The district has lost around 6,000 students since fall 2019 — enough to fill four high schools — and now educates about 42,000 students, in the same number of buildings. Many of those buildings are aging and in need of significant repairs.
Analysts with the Population Research Center at Portland State University, who review housing starts, birth data and historic rates of how many students in each neighborhood chose to attend public schools, predict that there will be 5,200 fewer students enrolled at PPS by 2035 than there are today. The overall 1-year median forecast error since 2010 is one half of a percentage point, according to PSU’s researchers.
Many unanswered questions remain about the scenarios, including exactly how many schools will make the final closure list. At the start of the school year, Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong said it could be between 15 and 20 schools.
Other unknowns: Whether at least four of the seven school board members will support the district’s proposal, how the shuttered buildings could be repurposed, and how many employees could lose their jobs as a result of the changes. Some teachers and support staff would likely transfer to new buildings alongside their students should the plans go through, but each building only needs one principal, for example.
Armstrong and other district officials have been hesitant to make firm promises about what schools would look like after consolidation, given their lack of control over state funding levels for education, local property tax collections and even who might win the gubernatorial election.
But they’ve said their goal and hope is for elementary schools to have multiple homerooms at each grade level, instead of blended classrooms, for school librarians, arts and PE teachers to be able to be full-time at a single school instead of splitting their time across multiple buildings and for middle school students to have access to a broad range of advanced courses and electives.
The Portland Association of Teachers has emerged as a potential key adversary to any change, questioning whether closing schools will exacerbate enrollment declines and if the school district has done enough to encourage families to choose PPS.
The district’s proposed scenarios will be presented to school board members Tuesday night. After that, there will be about five weeks for community feedback on the plans before Armstrong makes her final recommendation to board members in mid-November. The board’s final vote will come in mid-December.
The seven members of the school board all voted last spring to direct district staff to research closure scenarios, but that’s not the same as voting for a plan to close schools. Board members Christy Splitt and Stephanie Engelsman, in particular, have since expressed qualms about the scope and magnitude of the potential closures.
