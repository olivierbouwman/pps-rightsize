Guest Editorial by Steve Buel
Portland Public Schools is considering closing up to ten elementary schools next year. At first glance, it seems like a sensible thing to do with a budget shortfall of approximately $50 million. If you save a couple of million dollars at each school, then it would go a long way in reducing that deficit. But there is a problem. Maybe closing 10 schools won’t save any money. How so?
Many school finance specialists think no savings might be the result if you close a school. You still would have the same children to educate. You still would have to maintain the school building itself since municipal and fire codes wouldn’t allow you to just let it rot.
And as you increase the size of the new school the students would attend, associated costs would have to be taken into account. Would you need to remodel the new school to accommodate the new students? Would you need to add an extra administrator? Would you need to add classrooms? Would you have to increase the number of special education specialists and general classroom teachers since now you have more children with more complicated needs and just more students in general? How much would your transportation costs increase? These are just a few of the problems that could arise.
Before any school is closed, PPS needs to do a full, honest, and specific analysis that accurately outlines any money that might be saved. And it would need to be a lot of money saved because the social damage done to neighborhoods and the city could be enormous.
Neighborhoods are often built around the elementary school at its center. Closing the school can rip up a neighborhood. The area would no longer be as attractive to families. You no longer could walk your second-grade child down the street a block or two to their school.
If a school is closed, what replaces it most certainly would not be as conducive to a safe city lifestyle as
a nice little elementary school. Your housing values would fall.  Neighborhood children, your child’s playmates, would likely move elsewhere.
And there are educational problems, too. Your child and yourself need to start over at a new school. You won’t know most of the teachers and would likely have to adapt to the new school’s culture. In a way, it will be like your child is going to a new school.
There also may be other important, unaddressed problems. The reason for closing schools in Portland is said to be largely based on the size of the school. But the size of the school is determined by the boundaries of the school, something that the administration and school board totally control. It’s simple: increase your school boundaries and it is no longer a candidate for closure. This brings us to the critical issue of fairness.
For the last 35 years, Portland Public Schools has never measured the equality of the programs and opportunities at its individual elementary schools. Nor has it determined how equitable each school’s special programs – special reading and English Language Learning for example – might be. Without this information, the final decision on which schools to close could be incredibly biased.
One alternative to school closures would be to move all sixth grades back into the K-5 schools, making these schools much larger and actually improving the education. And if the district still determined some closure is needed, they could close a middle school or two with much less negative impact on the city and neighborhood children.
Steve Buel has been a teacher for 43-years and he is an ex-Portland School Board member.
