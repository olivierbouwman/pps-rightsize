As Portland Public Schools parents, we are alarmed by the severe budget crisis and the likelihood of neighborhood school consolidation. Access Academy is at odds with PPS’s commitment to equity and neighborhood schools (“Portland parents petition to close school for gifted students amid school consolidation talks,” Sept. 3).
Access serves approximately 320 Talented and Gifted children, about 8% of TAG-designated students. According to PPS’ data, this past school year, one Access student identifies as Black; zero identify as Native American; zero are English language learners and 75% come from Portland’s 11 richest zip codes, based on our calculations from a PPS public records request and Simplemaps.com.
While the percentage of white students in PPS has declined in recent years (currently 54%) it has risen at Access (currently 73%). In PPS, the five-year average of kids experiencing poverty is 26%; at Access, it’s 6%.
This isn’t surprising. Standardized test scores correlate with socioeconomic status, reflecting the inequalities public schools are tasked with redressing. At our neighborhood schools, many of our children’s classmates learn through housing insecurity, parental incarceration, and hunger. These kids are brave, goofy and resilient. They are brilliant in ways that standardized test scores won’t capture.
What do Access’ demographics tell us about who PPS considers “gifted” and worthy of extra support? How does this school reflect PPS’ stated goals of equity and inclusion? Why are schools like Access so rare nationally?
PPS will spend $3.4 million to operate Access this school year, and an additional $1.2 million to transport students out of their neighborhoods to Access. This money is better invested in our neighborhood schools where all students can benefit.
Michelle DuBarry, Portland
Emily Teplin Fox, Portland
Addie Humbert, Portland
The authors are parents of students at Ockley Green Middle School, Harriet Tubman Middle School, and Sabin Elementary School.
