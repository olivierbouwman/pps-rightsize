The case for a different path
8 facts to know about “rightsizing” and PPS
According to the district, “rightsizing” is our only choice to address declining enrollment issues and ensure PPS’ sustainability in the long term. While the challenges they cite are very real, we firmly reject the premise that “rightsizing” is the “best” or “only” option.
In the spirit of grounding the “rightsizing” discussion in the facts, here are some key truths to understand:
01
“Rightsizing” means closing up to 20 (or more) schools across PPS, resulting in larger class sizes and fewer options for families.
In spite of the optimistic name, “rightsizing” is an emerging trend among education policymakers that seeks to consolidate schools and increase class sizes, in the name of sustainability. It flouts decades of conventional wisdom and academic research that clearly demonstrate the merits of smaller class sizes and greater choice among schools.
Related source: PPS “rightsizing” FAQ. The proposed closure count and class-size outcome need a specific scenario citation.
02
Closing schools has an outsized, negative impact on both impacted students and surrounding communities.
Once a school closure starts, students suffer a well-documented learning loss process that slows their rate of learning and can often have long-term ramifications for their academic development.
Source supplied: UChicago, When Schools Close (2009), executive summary. The study found an announcement-year learning loss and average recovery afterward. It does not establish inevitable lasting harm or Portland’s future outcomes.
03
Vacant school buildings accelerate neighborhood disinvestment.
Time and again, closing schools, which typically serve as an anchor of a given neighborhood, creates additional hardships for communities – especially if they’re already struggling.
In defending “rightsizing”, PPS has consistently misrepresented and/or misinterpreted the data.
Throughout the process, PPS has cited Oregon’s Quality Education Model (QEM) as a guiding principle for “rightsizing” schools. However, the QEM’s prototypes recommend average class sizes of 20 students for elementary schools and 17.7 students for middle schools—a stark contrast to the 30+ student average class sizes that even the most conservative “rightsizing” process will yield.
Model reference: Oregon QEM 2026, printed p. 92. The prototypes use average class sizes of 20 (elementary) and 17.7 (middle), as recommendations rather than mandates. The projected PPS 30+ class size still needs a specific scenario citation.
05
The SEGC process is a cautionary tale for citywide “rightsizing”.
In recent months, both PPS and the School Board have cited the Southeast Guiding Coalition (SEGC), a process which saw the district redraw boundaries and feeder patterns, and relocate schools in Southeast Portland in 2023. Since that time, the following is true:
SEGC schools experienced larger enrollment declines than non-SEGC schools;
SEGC schools experienced larger populations of ESL and special education learners than their initial support projections anticipated;
SEGC school staff were broadly demoralized and left PPS at rates well above their peers elsewhere in the district, with staff of color leaving at nearly double the rate of their counterparts (18% vs. 10%).
Source needed: the SEGC evaluation, comparison periods, and tables supporting these enrollment, student-support, and staff-retention figures.
06
Jefferson County, Colorado is another highly relevant cautionary tale for Portland.
In 2025, Jefferson County proposed almost the exact same playbook, framed around “fewer but more robustly-resourced schools.” In a similarly sized district to PPS that was also dealing with an almost $60 million annual budget deficit, this process has only exacerbated their issues: the district has seen its budget gap increase even after selling vacant buildings, and announced 139 position reductions in December 2025 for the following school year (with 50 employees notified). The district has already dipped into reserve funds; critically, enrollment kept falling, even after the “rightsizing” process was put in place.
Reporting: Colorado Public Radio, December 19, 2025. Supports the announced job reductions and budget gap; it does not establish that school closures caused the gap to grow.
07
Success metrics are murky at best, and PPS keeps moving the goal posts.
According to the School Board during its September 1, 2026 open meeting, even in a post-“rightsizing” world, PPS won’t be in a place to consider “well-resourced” schools until at least the 2031-32 academic year. The goal in the interim is to get to “stability” – a marked contrast from its previous assurances that the “rightsizing” process would mean students would have better access to arts, gym and other extracurricular activities.
Source needed: the September 1, 2026 recording timestamp and presentation page for the quoted commitments and 2031–32 date.
08
“Rightsizing” is NOT our only option for charting a more sustainable path forward.
The district’s central office has repeatedly referred to “rightsizing” as the “only option” for charting a more sustainable path forward for PPS, which couldn’t be further from the truth. It’s a playbook that numerous districts across the U.S. have tried in recent years, usually with disastrous results.
While there’s no obvious “silver bullet” solution, a litany of options exist that can address PPS’ sustainability problem in a far less damaging way, among them:
Redrawing boundary lines;
Returning the PPS district office to pre-2019 staffing levels to maximize teacher resources;
Selling the district office and integrating staff above principal level into existing spaces in under-enrolled schools;
Investing more heavily in recruiting some of the many, many unenrolled students in PPS’ district boundaries.
These are proposed alternatives. Their costs, capacity, and feasibility need separate assessment.
→ The bottom line: This “rightsizing” proposal is being framed as the best and only option for PPS, when neither is true.
This entire process has been rushed, opaque and has consistently cherry-picked facts while ignoring the clear-cut data that shows we’re setting ourselves up for disaster.
It’s time to call on not only the school board, but also the Governor’s office to step in and ensure we stop this process before it’s too late to explore other, more truly sustainable alternative solutions.
