Title: Which Portland Public Schools buildings could be candidates for closure? Early clues are emerging
Outlet: The Oregonian/OregonLive
Author: Julia Silverman; Mark Friesen
Date: 2026-08-27

As Portland Public Schools board members debated the set of principles that will guide upcoming school closure proposals, one point came up again and again: No corner of the district is off-limits.
To that end, district analysts spent the summer weighing an extraordinarily complex set of factors, from the condition of each school building and its available classroom space to enrollment patterns present and future.
District leaders say they could ultimately close between 10 and 20 schools. They plan to provide more details on that number by mid-September and will release a list of potential closure scenarios in October.
“What we will say is that the scenarios will show the trade-offs,” Assistant Superintendent Margaret Calvert said. Some scenarios could prioritize neighborhood walkability, for example, but result in “split feeders” with students from the same elementary going on to different middle or high schools to balance attendance numbers — or vice-versa.
The district has been tight-lipped about its plan so far, but its guiding principles offer some clues about which schools could close.
District leaders are weighing a range of qualitative metrics such as school size, condition and usage. But they are also considering more complicated factors. That includes how to mitigate the impact on communities that have been shuffled around in the recent past, or have high numbers of historically underserved students.
Nicholas Verbon’s children are at Lent K-5, a school in east Portland that had its identity upended just a few years ago during an enrollment balancing process for about a dozen Southeast Portland schools.
In the transition, Lent, which receives extra funding from the federal government because of community poverty levels, became a Spanish immersion school; neighborhood students who were not in the Spanish program moved to nearby Marysville K-5 while students from the Spanish immersion program formerly housed at Bridger K-8 moved to Lent.
Since then, Verbon said, families there have been slowly but surely knitting their new community together.
“The principal is doing an amazing job,” he said. “The community has shown up for families during the increase in deportation and immigration enforcement. The school is becoming what it is designed to be: a full scale immersion program designed to serve the most vulnerable students.”
A PPS middle school teacher who has experienced the budget seesaw and loss of colleagues firsthand, Verbon said he understands why consolidation is under discussion, though he wishes the district could make more of an effort at recruiting back students who’ve left the system and that the state and federal government could prioritize more public school funding.
But he can’t help but worry that in a sure-to-be-cacophonous process, his school community will get drowned out.
“The most vulnerable families are not showing up to the Zoom meetings,” he said. “They might be working at night, or have internet connectivity issues or be single parents. So what does the outreach look like?”
Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong said she plans to be in schools regularly this fall, with pop-in visits at multiple buildings weekly, and at least two community meetings per week.
That said, Senior Chief of Operations Jon Franco cautioned that schools that educate higher concentrations of poor students or students of color aren’t exempt from the closure conversation. Instead, he said a major lesson learned from a district-commissioned report on previous changes is that the district must be prepared to offer above-and-beyond support to those communities to help manage complicated transitions as schools merge.
Districtwide, there are clusters of schools — some of which receive federal funding for high poverty levels and some not — that are close to each other and where one or both have space to take in new students with some possible boundary redraws, allowing for others to close, an analysis by The Oregonian/OregonLive shows. For a handful of schools within those clusters with very small buildings, even comfortably accommodating the 360-student elementary threshold the district is seeking would be a stretch.
Such elementary clusters might include:
- North Portland’s Chief Joseph and Peninsula
- North Portland’s Boise-Eliot-Humboldt and Beach
- Northeast Portland’s Sabin, Irvington and King
- Southeast Portland’s Whitman and Woodmere
- Southeast Portland’s Arleta, Creston and Marysville
- Southeast Portland’s Abernethy and Buckman
- Southeast Portland’s Lewis and Woodstock
- Southwest Portland’s batch of six elementary schools south of the Beaverton-Hillsdale Highway, including Hayhurst, Maplewood, Rieke, Capital Hill, Stephenson and Markham
But exactly which schools could be consolidated or closed within those clusters — or even if those schools will make the final list — is still unknown.
And the district has yet to specify what might become of vacant buildings, let alone how much it might cost to retrofit schools that will be admitting dozens or even hundreds of new students and what will happen to personnel that may find themselves out of a job as a result of consolidating.
For Creston Elementary School parent Sarah Horn, it all makes for a host of unsettling and yet unanswered questions.
Creston’s 78-year-old building is in poor condition and isn’t big enough to easily welcome hundreds of new students.
Though efforts by the previous principal to talk up the school at local preschools have paid off with two years of rising kindergarten enrollment, Creston had just 213 students last year. And five other elementary schools are located within a mile-and-a-half radius.
Clearer answers to what comes next, Horn said, will help her and other parents figure out how to feel. Otherwise, “knowing more budget cuts are down the line, it is hard to imagine things getting that much better.”
Focusing on focus options
A close read of the school board’s guiding principles suggests another group of schools that could be vulnerable to closures: The Portland versions of magnet schools, known as “focus options.”
Focus options typically offer a curriculum structured around a particular area, like math and science, the arts or a language. Those schools enroll students districtwide, as opposed to neighborhood schools, which are open only to those who live within delineated attendance boundaries.
Focus options can be very popular, with dozens more students hoping to enter than can be accommodated. But the school board’s guidelines make clear that they may not align with its primary mission.
“Neighborhood schools are the foundation of our school system,” school board members wrote in June’s guiding principles, and thus “ensuring access to strong, sustainable neighborhood schools will be a priority.”
Some focus options are also neighborhood schools that open extra space via a lottery to those outside their school’s attendance boundaries; others split buildings with neighborhood schools.
But a handful are accessed solely through a complex lottery system and typically siphon off students from nearby neighborhood schools. Transportation to the focus schools isn’t typically provided, an entry barrier for some families. Almost all focus option schools have lower poverty rates than districtwide averages.
Closing focus options alone probably is not enough to achieve the kind of broad enrollment parity Armstrong says she wants, but it could have an impact on particular communities that send especially large numbers of neighborhood students to focus schools.
For example, shutting down Winterhaven, a sought after K-8 math and science magnet in Southeast Portland, would theoretically return around 30 students to nearby Grout Elementary, boosting it over the district’s 360-student threshold. The same school could get another 30 students if the district chose to close Richmond Elementary, a popular Japanese immersion program that’s also within shouting distance of Grout.
But that strategy relies on the assumption that all focus option families would remain in the district, which is far from guaranteed, since some could opt instead for private options or move. A district that can’t control the significant enrollment impacts from declining birth rates or high housing costs will have to weigh whether that’s an additional risk it can afford.
“We need to look at which schools are doing really well and what are the models that are succeeding,” said parent Elissa Scott, who has two children at the Odyssey Program, a social sciences magnet in Southwest Portland. Rather than eliminate such schools, Scott said, the district should seek to expand their models and make them more readily available to more students.
In her community, Scott has sensed some fatalism about the months ahead and the decisions coming down the pike.
“The amount of instability it is creating for families and students is heartbreaking,” Scott said. “It’s hard to know as a parent what to do. There are people trying to get out of PPS because they feel something is coming and they want to get ahead of it.”
The middle school conundrum
Of the 11 neighborhood middle schools on the east side of the Willamette River, only two — Hosford and Sellwood, both in Southeast Portland — are just above the 500-student threshold where the district hopes to see every middle school, and perhaps not for long, given enrollment projections.
One reason why? The east side is home to eight of the district’s 11 remaining K-8 schools, mostly clustered in North and Northeast Portland.
That’s why looking closely at K-8 schools is another likely option for PPS as it seeks to plan different scenarios for board members and the public to consider.
For example, sending middle schoolers from North Portland’s Astor and César Chávez K-8s to nearby George Middle School instead would instantly boost enrollment there. The same would be true for redirecting Laurelhurst K-8’s middle schoolers to Mount Tabor Middle School or Beverly Cleary K-8’s middle schoolers to Beaumont Middle School.
Calvert was cagey with specifics, but did note when the district rebalanced enrollment across 20 Southeast Portland schools in 2023, one of the key changes was to open Harrison Park as a middle school, phasing out its previous identity as a K-8.
“These are levers that we have,” she said. “We’ll look at all of them.”
Closing or reconfiguring K-8s would have a domino effect, either opening up classroom space in former K-8s for elementary-aged students from other schools or making those schools candidates for closure.
For example, Faubion, one of the district’s newest buildings, seismically sound and air-conditioned, is highly unlikely to close. But moving its middle schoolers to Ockley Green Middle School could open space for more elementary students from nearby schools. Making space at Beverly Cleary, which is on tap for a $20 million seismic overhaul, could free up space for students from underenrolled Irvington, just over a mile away.
Lindsay Jensen, whose children attend César Chávez K-8 in North Portland, said parents there are highly aware of both the joys of a tight-knit K-8 community and the drawbacks of more limited middle school offerings.
There are people who’ve had students at the school for more than two decades, she said, and are firmly against any change. But others are more open to it, in search of better academic outcomes and more electives for their children.
“So many people feel a deep sense of connection here,” she said, adding that whatever happens, “we’re holding tight to see what PPS puts forward before settling on an advocacy strategy.”
