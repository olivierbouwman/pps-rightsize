Title: Editorial: Portland Public Schools’ narrow escape from budget myopia
Outlet: The Oregonian/OregonLive
Author: Editorial Board

You can’t fault Portland Public Schools board members for wanting to find some way to scrape together the $13 million needed to cancel the staggering layoffs of teachers, aides and other student-facing positions. But their panicked proposals last Tuesday to raid reserves or hastily swap administrator cuts for teachers offered a startling reminder of how quickly good intentions can turn reckless.
One board member, Stephanie Engelsman, sought to restore all $13 million simply by telling the district to do so. Another director, Rashelle Chase-Miller, proposed pulling money out of the district’s minimal savings, despite the potential hit to the district’s credit rating — a downgrade that would increase PPS costs to borrow money for school modernizations. Another director, Virginia La Forte, then suggested halving the amount that Chase-Miller sought in yet another attempt to see what spaghetti would stick.
Fortunately, reason won the day, thanks to objections from more seasoned board members and Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong’s patient explanations why such actions would only cause the district more pain. All but Engelsman, who abstained from the final vote, approved Armstrong’s budget, largely without changes, as The Oregonian/OregonLive’s Julia Silverman reported.
As excruciating as it is for the district to lay off employees, there are many tough decisions ahead for this board on school consolidations, boundary changes and new labor contracts. They must navigate the politics and pitfalls by remaining focused on students’ needs and the district’s long-term stability, rather than be swayed by in-the-moment desperation to retain teachers. Certainly, having more adults in classrooms is important for students’ success; but if that ultimately translates to fewer school days in an already truncated year, that is a tradeoff for employees’ benefit, not students’.
Additionally, board members should provide timely guidance and ask probing questions along the way, rather than leave their scrutiny to last-minute second-guessing. For example, they’re right to seek answers about how much the district spends on outside vendors, but that issue needed to have been raised long ago.
PPS board members should also recognize that even with a $56 million shortfall, the fiscal problems currently facing the district are driven primarily because expenses have grown much faster than revenue.
While some of that is due to state mandates, generous pension policies and an outdated funding formula, as Silverman’s recent reporting has shown, the fault also lies with PPS’ decisions in recent years to add new positions despite declining enrollment. PPS isn’t alone in scaling up its hiring, but school districts simply won’t escape this dynamic until and unless they make the structural changes necessary to bring budgets back into balance.

That’s why calls for the state to spend down its education savings fund are as shortsighted as proposals to reduce PPS’ reserves. Any such allocation is only a temporary balm that dodges the difficult restructuring that must take place.
That’s what Armstrong is trying to do, as she calmly explained to a school board that should know better already. In fact, she demonstrated impressive restraint and focus as several board members offered proposals that smacked of magical thinking or obliviousness.
Among them: a proposal to postpone a final vote to comb the budget for other cuts that would save teachers — as if a rushed review in the final days before it must be adopted would responsibly deliver substantial savings. They discussed directing Armstrong to come up with a plan for “aggressively” building up the reserves that they wanted to raid — as if the district hasn’t been forced to spend down its reserves ever since it passed a costly teachers contract in 2023. And it took multiple reminders from both Armstrong and Chief Financial Officer Michelle Morrison that the district is looking at an even larger shortfall for the 2027-2028 budget year to put the current challenges in perspective.
Such myopia isn’t limited to the Portland School Board. Portland City Council just adopted its budget for the 2026-2027 fiscal year with numerous provisions that raided savings accounts to tape together a spending plan when deeper structural fixes are needed.
The new budget year starts July 1. For both the city and the school district, elected officials who want to change the budget math for next year should take the long view and look for structural fixes now.
-The Oregonian/OregonLive Editorial Board
