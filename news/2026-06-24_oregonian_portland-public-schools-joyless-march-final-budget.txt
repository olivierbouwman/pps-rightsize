Title: At Portland Public Schools, a joyless march to a final budget no one much likes

The seven members of the Portland Public Schools board tried desperately to avoid signing off on a months-in-the-making $1.09 billion operating budget for the upcoming school year, before reluctantly casting their votes as the clock ticked toward midnight Tuesday.
In the end, six school board members voted in favor of the budget.
The lone hold-out was Zone 6’s Stephanie Engelsman, who had tried to amend the budget to come up with an extra $13 million to reverse the layoffs of more than 80 educators, most of whom found out in the final days of the school year that there would not be a job for them with the district when schools reopen in late August.
Engelsman abstained from the final vote.
But before that, board members spent roughly two hours brainstorming how and whether they could backfill the $13 million, in front of a watchful audience of leaders from and allies of the Portland Association of Teachers. The hail Mary of a discussion came after a months-long budget development process.
“I am here to beg you not to leave this dais until you’ve exhausted every single avenue to save educators,” Portland City Council member Tiffany Koyama-Lane, a former Sunnyside K-8 teacher, told board members. “Those student-facing positions need to be the very last positions to consider cutting.”
Among the potential fixes board members proposed and ultimately abandoned:
- Plans to dig into the district’s savings, taking that account below the 5% threshold that is mandated by board policy and directing Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong to “work aggressively” to build the fund back up in the coming months, perhaps by selling school district property. That fund currently stands at about $41 million, enough to keep the district’s operations afloat for a little less than a month in the event of an emergency; dipping into it could impact the district’s credit rating just as it is issuing a series of major construction bonds.
- A proposal to seek funding assistance from the city of Portland, which is itself facing a $160 million general fund shortfall that has required cuts to city jobs, shelter beds and public safety programs.
- A suggestion to ask corporations to pitch in between now and the start of the school year to sponsor teacher or counselor jobs.
- Asking the Fund for PPS to spearhead a last-ditch fundraising effort. The districtwide foundation for fundraising has struggled to gain traction with donors after a previous iteration of the board voted to phase out the ability for individual schools to raise funds to cover the costs of staff, which used to pay for about 20 teachers and teaching assistants districtwide.
- A top-to-bottom re-examination of thousands of outside vendor contracts.
- Leaving vacant jobs open or postponing hiring for a few months.
- Asking members of the Portland Association of Teachers to take across-the-board salary cuts in order to save their colleagues’ jobs.
- Renewed pleas to lawmakers to tap the state’s education stability fund. Both Gov. Tina Kotek and legislative leaders have cautioned against a distribution from that fund, given a huge pending reduction in federal support for Medicaid and food stamps that’s on track to wreak havoc with the rest of the state’s next biennial budget.
- Delaying a final vote on the budget for a week to give district leaders time to find more funding to save teaching jobs.
- Finding eleventh-hour cuts to district administrative positions, an oft-made suggestion from the teachers union which has repeatedly criticized management that it sees as top-heavy. Armstrong countered that the district’s central office has already absorbed $68 million in cuts over the past four years, which she said was proportionally far greater than cuts made in individual school buildings.
- Spending money now that has been set aside for staffing adjustments in the fall once firmed-up enrollment numbers shed more light on where more staff is most needed.
The board did agree to an earlier amendment offered by director Virginia La Forte to suspend discretionary out-of-state travel for school board members and senior district leaders for the next year, saving an estimated $77,000.
In response to the volley of last-minute ideas, Armstrong and Michelle Morrison, the district’s chief financial officer, consistently pointed to the projected budget cuts for the 2027-2028 school year, which they estimate will total $65 million, dwarfing this year’s $56 million in reductions. Any use of one-time funds now would only kick the figurative can down the road, driving next year’s cuts even higher, they warned.
Portland Public Schools is on the cusp of a systemic restructuring to better match expenses with funding, Armstrong said. The district has lost 12% of its students since fall 2019, due to a combination of falling birthrates, high housing costs and families choosing other schools. Projections say enrollment, which is tied to per-pupil state funding, will drop another 12% over the next 10 years.
In comparison, with approval of the new budget, the district will have about 7% fewer full-time employees than it did prior to the pandemic
Armstrong said that the district has a “structural deficit that has continued to build over time, and that’s not serving us well.”
“Reductions have been a part of Portland’s history for quite some time,” she continued. “My hope and my goal is to end that within the next two years.”
Ultimately, vice-chair Michelle DePass said, the board had simply run out of time to make significant changes.
“I don’t think much is going to change in seven days,” DePass said. “Sometimes we need to display courage and do hard things. We can’t say yes to everything that comes across our desk.”
