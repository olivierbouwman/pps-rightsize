Title: Portland Public Schools weighs what comes first: Seismic retrofits or school consolidations?

Portland Public Schools has a chicken-and-egg problem.
The school district is under pressure to quickly home in on which elementary and middle schools will be prioritized for seismic retrofits through a $100 million pot of money earmarked for that purpose in the $1.8 billion bond passed by voters last May.
But some of those same schools could be candidates for building closures as the district weighs what to do about declining enrollment. The dropoff in student counts has left Portland with more than 20 elementary schools that enroll between 200 and 300 students, in aging buildings that typically have space for around 500 students.
“We don’t want to go into a school and spend millions of dollars [on a seismic retrofit] just to have it be right-sized,” said Robert Jole, a senior project manager with the Office of School Modernization, at a school board subcommittee meeting last week. “Right-sized” is the district’s preferred catch-all term for the closure and merging of school campuses.
Decisions about school closures are fraught and don’t tend to move fast. That puts those tough conversations on a collision course with the pressure from parent advocates to move quickly to shore up buildings made from unreinforced brick masonry that are most likely to collapse in an earthquake.
At the school board’s facilities improvement and oversight subcommittee’s Oct. 8 meeting, Jole outlined the draft of a formula that the district’s staff could use to decide which schools will get a coveted piece of the $100 million in seismic retrofit funding.
The draft formula gives a 35% weight to an individual building’s seismic risk, and another 20% to its overall facility condition, meaning how much deferred maintenance work is built up there beyond seismic needs.
But the draft formula also assigns a 20% weight to its likelihood of being part of “right-sizing” — something that might be unknowable until district leaders and the school board decide which schools may close and which other schools would then absorb their student body.
The other factors include whether a school enrolls large groups of students whose academic outcomes are far below state targets and the age of students enrolled. The latter matters because, Jole said, middle and high school students are more likely than elementary schoolers to be able to follow directions to get themselves to safety in the event of an earthquake.
School board members will have the final vote on district-crafted closures and consolidation plans. Those conversations are expected to kick off in earnest next month in front of a separate subcommittee.
In the meantime, Jole said, some more minor seismic upgrades are planned for next summer at four schools: Cesar Chavez K-8, Vestal and Woodmere elementaries and Marshall High School, a swing site which will house students from Cleveland High School while their campus is being fully modernized beginning next fall. It is unclear whether those projects are being funded by the $100 million earmarked for seismic work in the 2025 bond, or by a separate stream of bond funding. The school district did not respond to repeated requests for clarification.
Full seismic retrofits on school buildings aren’t scheduled to begin until summer 2027, in part because the district hopes to secure grant funding from Business Oregon’s Seismic Rehabilitation Grant Program. That program has $50 million per year available for schools statewide and gives a maximum of $2.5 million per building.
Parent advocates for seismic safety say they’re frustrated both by what they see as the draft formula’s insufficient weighting of seismic needs and by the slow pace of the conversations.
Per Olstad, a Beverly Cleary K-8 parent who is a founding member of Safe Structures PDX, said that purely based on geography, it is unlikely that the majority of schools that are at highest risk of collapse in an earthquake will be selected for closure.
Those schools, as listed in a district-commissioned August report by the Holmes Consulting Group that assigned a seismic risk number to every district building, include Buckman, Rieke, Jason Lee and Rose City Park elementaries, Beverly Cleary K-8 and Jackson Middle School.
Rieke and Rose City Park have both been previously closed and repurposed, only to be later reopened as neighborhood schools. Rieke enrolls about 275 students, while Rose City Park has about 450, evenly split between neighborhood students and its dual language Vietnamese immersion program, which attracts students from around the city.
Jason Lee, which is on Northeast 92nd Avenue near Rocky Butte Natural Area, is relatively far from another elementary school and enrolls about 260 students. Buckman, a neighborhood arts magnet, is a little over a mile from both Abernethy K-5 and Sunnyside K-8 in inner Southeast Portland and enrolls about 300 students.
Olstad’s group is calling for seismic risk to have a significantly greater weight in deciding how and where the district decides where to put the $100 million it has set aside for retrofits, as per last spring’s resolution.
“When a third of the weight is given to seismic risk, it starts to feel like it is not really a prioritization based on seismic risk,” he said. “The resolution is clear that seismic is supposed to drive the formula, even if it is not the only factor.”
And Olstad said, the district could always present a plan for which schools will be retrofitted and specify that it is subject to change, based on consolidation decisions.
