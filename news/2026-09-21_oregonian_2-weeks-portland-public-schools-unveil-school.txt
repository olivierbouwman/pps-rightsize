Title: In 2 weeks, Portland Public Schools will unveil school closure scenarios. Some parents are already taking sides

In two weeks time, staff at Portland Public Schools will unveil their long-awaited scenarios for which combination of K-5, K-8 and middle schools they will recommend for closure.
Already, the process has exposed deep fault lines around the school district.
In Facebook groups, WhatsApp chats and via online petitions, some parents, in conjunction with the Portland Association of Teachers and its political allies, are organizing to stop any talk of closures in its tracks, or at least postpone decisions until there’s more clarity on the consequences.
In other corners, there are those who have concluded that some number of closed schools are inevitable, given deep enrollment declines and rising operational costs. They’ve pivoted to intense advocacy over which schools, or types of schools, should remain open.
In the middle are the seven disparate members of the Portland school board, a group that’s navigated very public divisions in the past 15 months, often acrimoniously. They will have the final say in a vote scheduled for December.
On Saturday, parents from across the city and representing multiple perspectives gathered at a community listening session at Faubion PK-8 in Northeast Portland with district staff and school board members Virginia La Forte and Stephanie Engelsman.
The only commonality that emerged was angst.
Kristen Kippen, whose daughter Eliza, 10, is in a fourth- and fifth-grade blend at Astor K-8 in North Portland, said she had originally sent Eliza to a Waldorf-inspired charter school but eventually switched her to their neighborhood school and never looked back.
“It shifted my whole perspective of what a community can be,” Kippen said. Talk of potentially phasing out K-8 schools to boost the district’s network of middle schools to at least 500 students apiece has been wrenching for her family, she said.
“It’s been so hard to know what is real and what is not,” Kippen said. “It feels like a lot of whiplash, a lot of spin. The lack of transparency means we are pitted against each other in a void.”
School district staff and board members said sessions like Saturday’s, more of which are scheduled in the coming days, are an attempt to demystify the process, which officially kicked off last spring but has come under especially intense public scrutiny since the school year began four weeks ago.
On Saturday, district staff offered information about the metrics they’re using to inform their decisions — enrollment goals and patterns, building conditions and footprints, geography, keeping students together with their classmates as they move from elementary school to middle school to high school and minimizing the impact on high-needs populations, including low-income communities and students of color.

But too many unanswered questions remain, parents said Saturday.
Andy Chiller, who has a first and a fourth grader at Arleta K-5 in Southeast Portland, said the district had been far too stingy with details on what would happen to shuttered school buildings.
“What happens when you have buildings costing the district money for security and maintenance?” Chiller asked. “Won’t that be a magnet for crime and problems in the community? Don’t rush into a decision that will create other problems.”
The district has said it is working on a plan for what happens to schools that close, but has issued few details. Some schools that were closed in previously have been repurposed as homes for public charter schools or programs, like the KairosPDX Learning Academy at the former Humboldt Elementary in North Portland, which has focused on eliminating the achievement and opportunity gaps for students of color, or ACCESS Academy, a program for talented and gifted students at the former Terwilliger Elementary in South Portland.
Others, like the former Edwards Elementary in Southeast Portland, are leased for use to organizations like Trillium Family Services, or have been sold to commercial ventures, like the Revolution Hall venue in Southeast Portland at the former Washington High School. And some, like Smith School in Southwest Portland, sit empty and are widely considered neighborhood eyesores.
Other parents, including Jessie Atherley, who has a kindergartner at Maplewood K-5 in Southwest Portland, said she wasn’t certain of the accuracy of enrollment forecasts that suggest Portland will lose another 6,000 students in the next 10 years, and said she wanted to ensure that the district had done everything it could to increase enrollment before resorting to closing schools. Barring that, she said, she was seeking guarantees on the educational experience that the remaining schools would be able to offer: Could the district promise full-time librarians, arts and PE teachers at all schools, for example?
Deborah Kafoury, the former Multnomah County chair who now serves as chief of staff to Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong, noted that Portland Public Schools has tried marketing campaigns to increase enrollment as well as redrawing attendance zones to better balance enrollment and shifting programs around, as recently as a few years ago in a Southeast Portland-specific effort. Kafoury said it has been “a lot of effort, without juice for the squeeze.”
Several of those at Saturday’s event, which drew about 50 people, belong to a group called Families for Equity in Schools, which has launched a lobbying effort called “Right-sizing the Right Way.” Their argument is that if closures are inevitable, the district should prioritize neighborhood schools and that the district should sunset “option” schools around the city, which are accessed via lottery and have a focus on a subject area like math or social studies.
“I would love not to have to close schools, but the population in the city indicates we have to do it,” said Nicholas Hengen Fox, who has one child at Sabin K-5 and one at Grant High School. “It would be excellent for my Sabin Elementary kid to be in a school that is full and has library, music, art, PE and Spanish teachers there every day. I think that is possible, and I would like PPS to commit more deeply to that model.”
“We should give PPS an opportunity to undo the harms caused by undoing a system that has given families the option to disinvest from neighborhood schools,” added Elizabeth Doerr, a Faubion PK-8 parent who is also an organizer with Families for Equity in Schools.
Across the room, Nick Biller, whose child attends Rieke Elementary in Southwest Portland, said he was involved with a group called Save PDX Schools that is seeking alternatives to closures.
“It’s not about pitting one school against another,” Biller said. “Every school is precious and fills important gaps. We can’t guarantee this will save any money, we can’t estimate class sizes or guarantee well-resourced schools — there’s just not a lot of transparency.”
La Forte, the board member, listened to it all and told parent after parent that there were no quick fixes left, and that if board members do nothing, resources that are already stretched paper-thin across 81 school sites will continue to dwindle. La Forte said her priority will be to get clear answers to community members’ many questions
“Everyone wants their school to stay, but the reality is that it isn’t going to happen,” she said. “I want to make sure that there is still a strong school at the center of our communities.”
