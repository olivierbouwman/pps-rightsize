Title: With enrollment declining and costs going up, Portland Public Schools tightens its belt — again
Outlet: The Oregonian/OregonLive
Author: Julia Silverman
Date: 2025-04-23
URL: https://www.oregonlive.com/education/2025/04/with-enrollment-declining-and-costs-going-up-portland-public-schools-tightens-its-belt-again.html

Oregon’s largest school district will operate next school year on a budget that is only about 1.4% more than the current year, requiring systemwide program and staffing cutbacks, Portland Schools Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong announced Tuesday night.
Hanging onto every program and every position would require an extra $40 million or so in its projected $1.09 billion day-to-day operations budget that the district does not have, Armstrong said. That pinch is due to the one-two punch of enrollment that’s declined 11% since 2019 and the rising cost of doing business, driven by inflation and the escalating costs of pay and benefits for employees, she said.
A school-by-school “heat map” that lays out the projected number of full-time employees that each school would lose shows a total of 157 school-based positions gone, including 32 fewer at high schools, 37 fewer at middle schools, 16 fewer at K-8 schools and 72 fewer at elementary schools, which have borne the brunt of enrollment losses, fueled by declining birth rates. The district has more than 7,000 total employees, meaning that it will lose about 3.5% of its positions.
Armstrong said earlier this year that school-based cuts were unlikely to result in layoffs, given vacancies and retirements, but that it was likely that some of the remaining staff members would wind up working in different schools come September.
It appears that fewer of the job losses would come from schools than she originally forecast when the outlines of the budget were first presented in January.Back then, Armstrong had suggested that schools absorb roughly 70% of the cutbacks, with the rest to come from the district’s headquarters.
In a presentation to school board members on Tuesday, Chief Financial Officer Michelle Morrison said that 44% of all the cost savings the district is proposing now come from the central office budget, while 56% would come from schools.
The second of the two nearly 300-page volumes detailing the budget released by the school system allows parents to look up their children’s schools and compare current staffing levels for all positions with next year’s projections.
Only a small handful of schools would add staff, including Benson High School and Roseway Heights Middle School in Northeast Portland, where enrollment is projected to grow, and Richmond Elementary in Southeast Portland, which has historically had some of the district’s largest class sizes.
Much more common would be losing educators and support staff. Schools that are projected to lose 10% or more of their teacher or other licensed staffers include Beach, Hayhurst, Capitol Hill, Lee, Markham, Marysville, Rosa Parks, Sabin and Woodstock elementaries, Bridger-Creative Science K-8, Kellogg, Lane, Mount Tabor and Beaumont middle schools and Jefferson high school.
The impact of the lost positions will be felt in fourth and fifth grade classrooms, where the district is encouraging blended grade levels as a way to keep class sizes small for younger students, and at middle and high schools, where classes would have to enroll at least 15 students to make it onto the schedule. Armstrong’s budget plan also proposed to cut support staff for dual language middle school students.
On Tuesday, Armstrong told school board members that roughly 85 central office-based employees will be laid off, including 14 whose positions had been funded by grants, and that other savings would come from eliminating contracted work. She said some of the impacted jobs would include those who have worked on staff professional development or who provide translation and interpretation services districtwide to families who don’t speak English.
At Tuesday’s board meeting, school board members voted 6-1 to increase the salary schedule for “senior chief” leadership, making it possible for people in those positions to be paid between $224,000 and $239,000. Armstrong has appointed two “senior chiefs:” Kimberly Howard, newly promoted to senior chief of academics, and Jon Franco, newly promoted to senior chief of operations.
Armstrong said creating those new higher-tier positions while eliminating some of the directors positions they supervise will ultimately result in cost-savings as she seeks to streamline central office operations.
The dissenting vote came from outgoing school board member Julia Brim-Edwards who said she was concerned that it added an extra administrative layer to the district’s organizational chart and worried about adding to leadership salaries while cutting teaching positions.
Armstrong and Franco will supervise 72 other chiefs, senior directors and directors, including some who oversee non-academic programs like nutrition, security, transportation and the office of school modernizations. That’s down from 82 people in such positions this year, Armstrong said.
Looming over the budget process is the question of federal funding. The Trump administration has threatened to pull funding that supports schools that serve high numbers of low-income families, special education and school nutrition programs over a dispute about the state’s support for diversity, equity and inclusion programs.
The district plans to hold another budget work session with public comment on April 29 at Grant High School from 6 p.m. to 8 pm.
Armstrong also warned Wednesday that the 2026-2027 year would bring a fresh round of cuts — around another $32 million. Some of this year’s cuts were offset by the use of one-time funds, like a reserve fund that had been previously set aside to address cost overruns in the Public Employee Retirement System, Armstrong said, which can’t be tapped again next year.
Armstrong and school board members alike have said that the district is likely to consider school consolidations and redrawing of enrollment boundaries in the next year to respond to decreasing enrollment levels, which are projected to keep declining over the next eight years.
The district has 24 elementary schools and K-8 schools that are projected to enroll fewer than 300 students next year, including some that are within a mile of each other; Jefferson High School’s enrollment is projected to be around 430 students.
