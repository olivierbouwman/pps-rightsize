So we should close schools?

Closing schools saves the district a little, but harms 
students and communities a lot.
Research on Chicago school closures found students lost roughly 1.5 months of reading progress during 
the announcement year alone, before any transition occurred. 
Philadelphia research shows receiving schools experience measurable achievement declines for up to 
two years after absorbing displaced students, particularly when they exceed a ~10% concentration threshold. 
Once holding costs, security, and maintenance on vacant buildings are factored in, net savings typically fall 
below $1M per building — a modest return for significant community disruption (source). Moreover, recent 
research from California shows that district savings from school closures were offset by revenue losses from 
losing enrollment during the closures.
Vacant school buildings accelerate neighborhood disinvestment. research by Brinig & Garnett links 
closures to increased blight, crime, and erosion of the social cohesion that schools anchor in their 
communities. 
Long-term data from Milwaukee and Texas show students displaced by closures have lower graduation 
rates, lower college enrollment, and lower earnings into their mid-20s when they don't land in 
meaningfully better schools.

Cautionary Tale: Southeast Guiding Coalition (SEGC)
PPS commissioned an evaluation of their SEGC work that redrew boundaries, feeder patterns and relocated 
schools in 2023.
SEGC schools experienced larger enrollment declines than non-SEGC schools, which was part of what led to 
more “balanced” enrollment. (p. 6)
SEGC-impacted schools experienced a concentration of student need, with larger populations of English 
learners and students with disabilities than projected after the project. (p. 6) 
Although a stated goal of SEGC was to provide access to robust middle school experiences, SEGC middle 
school outcomes were not transformed. (p.43)
School staff were broadly demoralized and left PPS at rates well above their peers elsewhere in the district, 
with staff of color leaving at nearly double the rate of their counterparts (18% vs. 10%). (pp. 14, 17) 


Cautionary Tale: Jefferson County, Colorado (“Jeffco”)
Jeffco did exactly what PPS is proposing, calling it "Jeffco Thrives 2025," framed around "fewer but more 
robustly-resourced schools." Their problems aren’t solved:
● Despite saving $12-20M/year and selling buildings, Jeffco announced a $60M budget gap for 
2026-27 in Dec 2025, cutting 139 more positions (50 layoffs) and dipping into reserves. (CPR News, 
Dec 2025)
● One round wasn't enough: beyond the original 16 schools, Jeffco closed two more K-8s in 2024, 
ended its high-school moratorium, and is now closing Jefferson Jr./Sr. High for 2026-27, with 18 
schools still under 60% capacity (28 projected by 2027-28). (Chalkbeat, Feb 2025)
● Enrollment kept falling: Jeffco lost 4,200+ more students (5%) after the closures. (CPR News, Dec 
2025)


Other districts avoided closures
San Francisco: proposed 13 elementary + 2 high schools for closure; the plan 
was halted entirely within weeks of public pushback, the superintendent resigned, 
and any closures were pushed to 2026-27 under a rebuilt process 
Seattle: proposed closing 21 elementaries, ultimately closed zero for 2025-26; 
projected savings shrank from $30M to $5-6M once specific schools were named, 
and enrollment had already stabilized by the time the plan was withdrawn.

WHAT THE  QEM ACTUALLY IS
360

500

Three hypothetical "prototype schools," used only to estimate the 
statewide cost of a quality K-12 system. It is a funding-formula 
input, not an evaluation of any real school or district.
HOW PPS IS USING IT
15
elementary schools recommended for closure so every 
remaining elementary reaches 360 students
The plan takes the headcount half of the QEM's prototype and 
leaves the resourcing half undefined: PPS has not committed to 
funding a nurse, librarian, PE specialist, and music specialist at 
every consolidated school, the actual substance the QEM says 
"360" is supposed to buy. 
“...the AIR report recommended increasing the number of 
prototype schools to better represent diverse student needs and 
district characteristics… the QEM remains a tool designed for 
statewide estimation."
— Quality Education Commission, 2026 report
