With the current school year almost at its halfway point, school districts around Oregon have begun looking ahead to next year.
The picture isn’t pretty.
In Portland, district leaders have released an early draft of how they would close a $50 million budget gap that includes 288 fewer employees overall, including 180 fewer employees in the district’s 81 school buildings and 108 fewer staff members in its central office, many of whom juggle assignments in multiple schools.
That $50 million represents about 5% of the district’s $1.09 billion current year operating budget. The school district has about 5,800 employees, meaning it will shrink its workforce by about 5%.
District leaders have stressed that the proposal is in its earliest stages and will shift and change after feedback from all corners, including at four community listening sessions set for this week, at McDaniel High School and Harriet Tubman Middle School on Wednesday evening and Cleveland and Ida B. Wells High Schools on Thursday night.
In a message to families, Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong wrote that the district’s focus will be on “protecting required services, paying close attention to class size and carefully reviewing impacts to programs students rely upon,” as final budget decisions get made.
The fresh round of potential cutbacks comes after the district made $40 million in cuts for the current school year, which caused a shift to blended classrooms in fourth and fifth grades, sunsetting programs including its language acquisition department and larger class sizes in middle and high schools.
Portland is the state’s largest school district, but it is not alone in projecting cuts for next year. Salem-Keizer, the state’s second-largest school district, says it is preparing for $25 million in cuts, after making $70 million in cuts just two years ago. The Eugene School District has outlined plans to reduce its budget by $30 million, including the elimination of up to 159 jobs.
Other large metro area districts, including Beaverton, Hillsboro and Tigard-Tualatin, have yet to release their specific budget proposals for next year. All school districts are required to get board approval and adoption of their budgets by the end of June.
The seemingly never-ending parade of cuts is caused by several underlying factors:
- A pronounced drop in enrollments, which have leveled off since the pandemic but have not returned to pre-pandemic levels, and are unlikely to do so, given declining birth rates. In Oregon, dollars follow students. In the 2023-2024 school year, per-pupil expenses were about $18,400 in state and federal dollars, according to the state Department of Education.
- A concurrent increase in expenses, from labor costs agreed to during contract negotiations to purchasing equipment and curriculum.
- The rising costs of special education and behavioral health needs, as more students arrive at schools with complex needs. Oregon caps extra funding it gives to offset the costs of special education at 11% of a school’s student body, even though some school districts have up to 20% of their students needing such services.
- A pending spike in payments due to the Oregon Public Employees Retirement System, as so-called side accounts that many school districts relied upon to fuel their obligations to the retirement system are set to expire.
- Dwindling reserves at some — but not all — school districts, which can be tapped to cushion shortfalls, though that’s a dangerous strategy since those are one-time funds and that only kicks the budget can down the road. Portland has only about $41 million in its reserve funds and using it all would downgrade the district’s credit ratings and leave it without any savings at all in case of emergency.
In their preliminary document, Portland Public Schools budget officials offered some granular detail about where they would make the bulk of their reductions. Fifty-one school-based jobs would be eliminated because of enrollment declines across grade levels and another 49 by creating more blended classrooms across K-5 and K-8 schools, including in grades 1 and 2. That’s a continuation of a strategy that the district began experimenting with this year, with more blends among grades 4 and 5.
Meanwhile, class sizes would increase in middle and high schools, meaning that fewer sections of different classes will be offered, since there will be fewer educators overall available to teach.
Another big change the district proposed is to cut back on instructional coaches, a big investment made during the pandemic intended to support classroom teachers with designing lessons and meeting individual student needs. There is some proposed wiggle room for principals to add back instructional coaches or morph that position to better meet the needs of their building.
The reductions do not necessarily mean that current staff will be let go. Typically, many reductions can be absorbed by retirements and attrition, but it does mean that there will be fewer total adults employed by the school.
The proposals come as the district prepares to head into contract negotiations at the end of this month with the Portland Association of Teachers and its other employee unions.
In an email to the 3,200 members of the union sent last week, Portland Association of TeachersPresident Angela Bonilla said the district had increased its spending on administrative costs more quickly in years past than its rate of spending on building-level educators.
But she also put blame on state lawmakers and Gov. Tina Kotek, writing that the Legislature had “refused to increase funding equivalent to the increase in costs,” and that the governor should call for spending down the more than $1 billion currently in the state’s education stability fund, which is the state’s rainy day reserves.
