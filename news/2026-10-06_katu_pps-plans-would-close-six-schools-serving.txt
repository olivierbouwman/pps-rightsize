PPS plans would close six schools serving more low-income families
PORTLAND, Ore. (KATU) — Portland Public Schools is weighing two closure plans, but both would shut the same six Title I schools.
Three of the schools are in the Jefferson/Roosevelt region, while the other three are in the Cleveland/Franklin region. None of the proposed Title I closures are in Grant/McDaniel or Wells/Lincoln.
Title I gives schools extra federal money to help students learn. It supports schools serving families with needs such as poverty, although a Title I label does not mean every child at the school lives in poverty.
Title I schools represent 43% of proposed closures in Plan A and 55% in Plan B
Cleveland/Franklin would have the most closures overall: six under Plan A or five under Plan B. Jefferson/Roosevelt would lose four schools under either plan.
Grant/McDaniel and Wells/Lincoln would each lose two schools under Plan A or one under Plan B.
According to publicly available data, three schools in Jefferson/Roosevelt enrolled 892 students in 2025–26. The three in Cleveland/Franklin enrolled 817 students. That adds up to 1,709 students at the six schools.
Those numbers count every student at the schools. They do not show how many live in poverty or how many would need a new school when the changes begin. The figures come from adding the schools’ historic enrollment in the PPS and Portland State University report.

The two regions also have the most district-run schools receiving Title I money.
Across PPS, 35 district-run schools and two district-sponsored charter schools are on the Title I funding list. Together, those 37 schools enrolled 13,653 students. That includes four schools receiving temporary “bridge” funding after leaving Title I eligibility. The six schools proposed to close account for about one in eight students attending those 37 schools.
Gabrielle Haber, a Sunrise Elementary parent and member of the Title I Coalition, worries about the strain on families.
“But when they're giving us scenarios where six out of 14, six out of 11 schools are Title I communities, and they're concentrated in specific areas of our city, that's not equity,” Haber said.
She said getting to school and keeping trusted relationships with school staff matter for students.
“And for the kids themselves, it really matters that they have those relationships at school that make them keep wanting to come back,” she said.
PPS says fewer children are enrolling, leaving some schools too small to support steady staffing and a full range of programs. It says combining schools could help provide more consistent access to art, music, libraries and counselors.
The district says it will work with affected families and staff to plan transitions and review whether some communities face greater impacts. Portland School District.
Haber wants details about how each school’s support would follow its students.
“And so we just, we really need to see that concrete plan of how those specific resources are going to be carried through in the transition,” she said.
PPS says the superintendent will make a formal recommendation in November, with a board vote in December. Changes would begin in fall 2027.
