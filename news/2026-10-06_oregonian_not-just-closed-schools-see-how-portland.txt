Leaders at Portland Public Schools will make their case for why they identified 14 schools across the city as potential candidates for closure during what is expected to be a standing-room-only school board meeting Tuesday night.
Their presentation to board members — who ultimately have the final say over whether any schools will close and if so, how many — comes just 24 hours after the district publicly released two scenarios outlining potential closures that could take effect by next fall.
That release has touched off a firestorm of frustration and follow-up questions among parents and staff, some of whom have already launched an all-out effort to persuade school board members to delay or defer any closures, boundary changes or program moves.
District staff, led by Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong, say that consolidating schools makes academic and financial sense in a district that has lost 6,000 students in the past seven years.
In materials prepared for Tuesday’s board meeting, the district said that under “Scenario A,” the most far-reaching of its proposals which would close 14 schools, 80% of students would attend a “sustainable” school, defined as a K-5 school with around 360 students and a middle school with around 500 students. The district says schools of that size allow for fewer blended grade levels, more electives and advanced academic courses and fewer specialists — like art and gym teachers, or librarians – who must split their time between different school buildings.
If school board members opt for Scenario B, under which 11 schools would close, 73% of students would attend a sustainable school, according to the district. Making no changes would mean that 48% of the district’s students would be in schools that meet the sustainability threshold going forward.
Here’s a closer look at the sweeping changes proposed by the district under both scenarios. Under Scenario A, 27% of all students enrolled in Portland Public Schools — or about 11,000 in total — would be impacted by the changes. Under Scenario B, that number would drop to 10,080.

Armstrong and Senior Chief of Operations Jon Franco outlined more of the thinking behind the proposed scenarios in a memo to board members.
“In the Roosevelt and Jefferson region, dual language immersion consolidation and middle school enrollment were primary drivers,” Franco and Armstrong wrote.
Elsewhere on the east side, the focus shifted, they wrote, to converting K-8 schools to K-5 models, plus reducing the number of so-called “split feeders,” particularly middle schools whose students graduate and go to different high schools.
And across the Willamette River on the west side, Franco and Armstrong wrote, geographic isolation, travel distances and elementary school enrollment emerged as central considerations.
Key implementation details, such as how consolidated schools would handle transportation, staffing and extra supports to help children make the transition will require “further implementation planning,” the two wrote.
Tuesday’s board meeting will kick off two and a half months of what is expected to be a fierce debate over the district’s proposals.
Armstrong will give board members her recommendation for which scenario she believes will best serve the district, its students and the city in mid-November. A final board vote is expected by mid-December. Board members could accept Armstrong’s recommendation, decide to tweak it, or make no changes at all.
