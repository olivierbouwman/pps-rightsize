Title: As Portland Public Schools weighs sweeping closures, parents wonder which schools will be left standing

As she prepared to send her kids back to Creston Elementary School this fall, Sarah Horn said she and her family couldn’t quite shake the feeling that they were sitting ducks.
Like thousands of other families around Oregon’s largest district, they’re waiting to see if their small school will make Portland Public Schools’ list of buildings proposed for closure or consolidation.
Over the next three months, they’ll get their answer, the result of a politically and emotionally charged process that’s set to fundamentally reshape the district and could ultimately affect students, families and staff in up to half of its 81 buildings.
District leaders have avoided portraying the plan as a response to budget pressures alone. Instead, they say closures of elementary and middle schools will allow them to expand opportunities for students and stabilize schools as they navigate the one-two punch of rapidly declining enrollment and spiraling operating costs.
“If only they could say that by doing this, we’ll save XX dollars, and your kids will be guaranteed all the (art and music) specials, all the hours, more assistance in the classroom,” Horn said. “But we have yet to hear any hard examples or cost savings and benefits of this effort.”
Figuring out which schools to propose closing is an extraordinarily complex calculation, requiring district analysts to balance a stew of factors both tangible — enrollment patterns, transportation logistics, building condition and capacity — and abstract, including how to avoid outsized impacts on the system’s most vulnerable students.
Add to that the high-wire political stakes, particularly for Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong, now starting her third year in Portland.
In an interview with The Oregonian/OregonLive, Armstrong said the district will provide more details by mid-September about how many buildings might make the closure list, and how many others will face attendance boundary changes or wind up with two programs housed within the same building.
The following month, Portland Public Schools will release a list of potential closure scenarios for community members and the school board to chew over, much as it did during last fall’s contentious conversations over redrawing attendance boundaries around Jefferson High School.
That should touch off about a month to six weeks of emotionally charged feedback and lobbying before Armstrong issues her preferred scenario and school board members vote in early December. Buildings could begin closing in fall 2027.
Armstrong previously suggested that between five and 10 schools could close. But in August, she told The Oregonian/OregonLive that the number could be well into the double digits.
Her path is littered with the ghosts of big-city superintendents who’ve tried and failed to pull off similar right-sizing and lost their jobs for their trouble.
In Seattle, former Superintendent Brent Jones exited in March 2025, just months after plans to close up to 21 schools collapsed amid public fury over district leaders’ perceived failure to specify how much their plans would save, how teachers would fare and what would happen to special education services, bus routes and after-school care for impacted students. Today, Seattle Public Schools is still facing a $100 million budget shortfall and its teachers union is threatening to strike over pay increases.
In San Francisco, former Superintendent Matt Wayne resigned in fall 2024 after a botched rollout of closure plans touched off recriminations from all sides, including school board members and the city’s then-mayor, London Breed. Debate over the closures was short-lived, with school officials retreating to focus on rebalancing the budget. The district’s current leaders now say any further discussion of closures will need to wait until at least 2030.
If Armstrong felt she had any other choice, presumably she’d take it.
But in her view, Portland Public Schools, which has been whipsawed by painful budget reductions over the past four years and faces an estimated $65 million in cuts for the 2027-28 budget, must restructure. Doing so, Armstrong says, will provide PPS the stability that’s eluded it for years.
Demographers at Portland State University predict that due to high housing costs, fewer people moving to the city, low birth rates and some families choosing online or private schools, PPS enrollment will continue to fall, reaching a low of about 36,000 students by 2039. That’s 15% fewer students than currently enrolled.
“We’re seeing that we could be at a place where every grade in a school is blended because there aren’t enough full-time employees to be able to support single grade level classrooms, where enrollment has decreased so far that we have some of our educators split between three sites,” Armstrong said. That’s not what parents and teachers want, she said, nor what students deserve.
Other Oregon districts will be watching closely. Just to the west, Beaverton has been slow-rolling a community conversation about closures after heated backlash from parents; to the south, Salem-Keizer has announced that it will attempt to close up to eight schools in the next two years. And in Newberg, after the failure of a local option levy last spring, the school district has convened a parent advisory committee to weigh options for restructuring.
Mindful of public backlash that greeted similar messaging in Seattle, Armstrong and her deputies have so far scrupulously avoided selling Portland’s plan to close schools as primarily a money-saver and have not released a cost savings estimate.
“Of course we envision some semblance of operating savings,” Senior Chief of Operations Jon Franco said. “But is it going to make up for the level of deficits we are going into? No. We have never said this is about saving money. It is truly about the consistency of student experience across the board.”
The district’s goal is to have elementary schools with at least 360 students and middle schools with at least 500, said Associate Superintendent Margaret Calvert, who is running point on the resizing efforts.
That’s the threshold set by the state’s Quality Education Model, which outlines what it would theoretically cost to operate an ideally-sized school in Oregon. Calvert said it should be big enough to allow for two teachers per grade level, arts and gym teachers who don’t have to divide their time between campuses, and multiple electives for middle schoolers.
Right now, there’s a 400-student gap between the district’s smallest elementary school, Rosa Parks in North Portland, and its largest, Ainsworth in Southwest Portland. There’s a similar-sized gulf between the smallest middle school, George in North Portland, and its largest, Jackson in Southwest, making for vastly different experiences for students.
If the district can pull off its closure plan, Armstrong said, “We’ll continue to have some (enrollment) decline over the next several years. But then typically you have retirements every year, so it won’t result in massive layoffs and closing of programs.”
The district’s exact proposals remain under lock and key, for now.
But late last June, school board members agreed upon a handful of priorities to guide staff working on the plans. It garnered little notice as the school year ended three days early due to midyear budget cuts and after a rash of last-minute layoff announcements.
Chief among those priorities: Schools should have enough students to offer “well-resourced and sustainable” programming. Other considerations include maintaining safe and up-to-date buildings, taking special care with schools that serve large numbers of students of color and those from poorer families and emphasizing neighborhood schools.
To make its case, the district has published maps with its schools’ enrollment levels and “utilization rates,” which measure how many students are enrolled versus capacity.
Not included on the published maps — but important — are the school district’s “facility condition index” rankings. That metric assigns a rating to every building based on its physical condition and how much it would cost to renovate versus rebuild from the ground up.
Only two of 41 K-5s and one of nine K-8 schools have facility condition rankings that are publicly categorized as “good”: Rosa Parks and Forest Park K-5s and Faubion, which serves preschoolers through middle schoolers. Another seven elementary schools — James John, King, Peninsula, Sabin, Scott, Woodlawn and Woodstock — are rated as being in “fair” condition, along with one K-8, Beverly Cleary.
Given the district’s huge deferred maintenance backlog, district officials have said that PPS is unlikely to mothball schools that don’t need millions of dollars in repairs, though the grade configurations or attendance boundaries there could still change.
On the flip side, the three schools categorized as being in “critical” condition – Glencoe, Llewellyn and Chapman K-5s — aren’t necessarily automatic closure candidates, given relative geographic isolation in some cases, and whether nearby schools have room to absorb their students in others. And with fewer operational buildings, schools in poor or critical condition that remain open could climb up the priority list for future repairs.
Another school unlikely to be affected is Skyline K-8, whose remote location along Forest Park’s western edge would make busing its several hundred students elsewhere prohibitively expensive.
District analysts are balancing an extraordinarily complex number of factors as they weigh their recommendations. Along with the broad guidelines, attention must be paid to everything from preserving dual language programs and anticipating where new housing starts are projected to avoiding the fracturing of existing school communities and preventing 5-year-olds from crossing dangerous intersections on their way to school.
It all adds up to a host of unanswered questions, including how much pushback the district can expect the powerful Portland Association of Teachers to mount should it become clear that closures will result in an initial wave of educator and staff layoffs.
“As a parent, I want to be open and excited about more opportunities for our kids,” Horn said. But the multitude of unknowns is making that difficult, she added.
