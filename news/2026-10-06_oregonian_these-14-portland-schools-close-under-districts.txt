Title: These 14 Portland schools could close under district's consolidation plan

Portland Public Schools on Monday unveiled two potential scenarios for a wave of school closures, boundary changes and program shifts that would fundamentally reshape the district, directly impacting students, teachers, staff and families at up to 42 of its 81 schools.
The two scenarios will be presented to school board members Tuesday evening and are likely to be the subject of fierce debate over the next two and a half months, with the influential Portland Association of Teachers union shaping up as a pivotal adversary seeking to derail or delay any closures.
Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong will give board members her recommendation for which scenario she believes will best serve the district, its students and the city in mid-November. A final board vote is expected by mid-December. Board members could accept Armstrong’s recommendation, decide to tweak it, or make no changes at all. Any changes would begin taking effect next fall.
Scenario A is the more far-reaching of the two proposals and would close 14 schools, including 13 elementary schools and Sellwood Middle School in Southeast Portland.
The 13 elementary schools that would close under that plan are: Maplewood and Stephenson in Southwest Portland; Buckman, Creston, Marysville, Woodmere and Lewis in Southeast Portland; Irvington, Rose City Park and Sabin in Northeast Portland; and Beach, James John and Peninsula in North Portland.
Under Scenario B, 11 schools would close, with Stephenson, Lewis and Rose City Park moving off the closures list.
Six of the 14 schools proposed for closure are Title I schools, which receive extra funding from the federal government because of higher poverty rates among the families of their students: Buckman, Marysville, Woodmere, Beach, James John and Peninsula. All save Buckman enroll a majority of students of color.
The share of Title I schools impacted by the proposed closures roughly matches the share of those schools across the district overall.
The district had previously faced criticism when it made significant boundary changes and program shifts to high needs schools throughout parts of Southeast Portland in 2023 while leaving whiter and wealthier schools intact.
Under both proposals, some middle schools would become less economically diverse, including Beaumont in Northeast Portland, and Hosford in Southeast Portland, but overall, the number of middle schools with students branching off to different high schools would drop, which was one of the district’s goals.
In an interview with The Oregonian/OregonLive, Armstrong said district staff do not plan to add any additional schools to the closure list between now and the board’s vote in mid-December, but she cautioned that board members will have leeway to direct them to do so.
“I can’t promise that they won’t have questions about schools that aren’t listed,” she said. “We could hear some feedback from Scenario A that will lead to adjustments, and then for B, maybe some adjustments, and out of that comes the recommendation. And if they decide to defer the decision, I’ll respect the choices of the board. I just know that we’ll be back in front of them with a conversation each year for the next several years.”
Armstrong said her hope is to avoid a rerun of such emotionally charged conversations by adopting a big enough change now that the district won’t have to endure another round of closures for at least another five or six years.
Urban school districts across the country are grappling with school closures, fueled by the same factors as in Portland: Enrollment declines, primarily driven by falling birthrates and high housing prices that have made metro areas unaffordable for young families. That’s coupled with ever-rising operational costs, from salaries and benefits to the gas to power school buses. Portland Public Schools has lost 6,700 students since 2019, enough to fill four high schools. Enrollment this fall is down an estimated 700 students from the previous school year, an ominous sign in a state where funding is pegged to enrollment.
That cold hard reality, Armstrong and other district leaders say, translates to too many school buildings housing too few students, spreading staff thinner and thinner. High schools have paid the price, with larger classes and fewer elective choices so that money could instead go to keeping average class sizes in the mid-20s in elementary schools, according to Margaret Calvert, the district’s director of school planning and modernization.
About half of the district’s 45 elementary schools now enroll fewer than 300 students, forcing grades to be blended, electives to be cut, and librarians, art teachers, gym teachers, counselors and other specialists to divide their time between schools.
In just the past few weeks, Minneapolis Public Schools has proposed closing up to 16 schools, Tulsa Public Schools has floated a plan to close 17 schools and Buffalo Public Schools in New York has said it is considering shutting eight schools. Closer to home, the Salem-Keizer School District will publicly unveil its plan to close up to eight schools by the end of this week.
In Portland, both proposed scenarios would accelerate the district’s years-in-the-making drive to phase out K-8 schools, in favor of larger, standalone middle schools, which officials say offer a broader range of electives and advanced coursework, and make entering a comprehensive high school less of a culture shock. The district has said its goal is to have at least 500 students per middle school and 360 in each elementary school.
Seven current K-8 or PK-8 schools would become K-5 schools under both scenarios: César Chávez in North Portland; Faubion, Vernon, Beverly Cleary and Laurelhurst in Northeast Portland; and Sunnyside and Bridger Creative Science in Southeast Portland. Sunnyside, which has housed an environmental justice magnet school, would become a neighborhood K-5 school instead.
There is one exception: Metropolitan Learning Center, an alternative option in Northwest Portland, would shrink from a K-12 to a K-8 and begin sharing its space with the Odyssey Program, a K-8 program with a social studies focus that has been located for several years in the former East Sylvan K-5 in Southwest Portland.
With so many shifts, the school district would still need to ensure that some schools are not bursting at the seams while others sit half-empty.
The district is proposing doing that in part by consolidating language immersion programs into fewer schools, particularly for Spanish. For example, in North Portland, César Chávez K-8 would transform into a “neighborhood hub” for several different Spanish K-5 immersion programs, hosting students from Beach, Sitton and James John elementaries. That could surface complicated transportation logistics for three high-poverty schools. James John is two miles from César Chávez’s campus, and Sitton and Beach are each about three miles away.
Both scenarios leave intact some other, very popular and typically less demographically diverse magnet schools, known in Portland as “focus options” because they have a particular concentration on a language or a subject matter, like math or the arts. Families who want their children to attend those schools must enter a lottery to do so and hope their number gets pulled. The schools that were spared include Da Vinci Middle School, an arts magnet, and Winterhaven K-8, a science magnet, which are in extremely high demand, judging by waitlist numbers.
Armstrong said the district’s thinking was to retain at least some such schools, which have historically served as calling cards for families seeking choices beyond their neighborhood schools.
At least a handful of the schools on the closure list were impacted by the district’s most recent efforts to balance enrollment, when boundaries were redrawn and programs were moved in much of Southeast Portland for the 2023-24 school year. Some are still feeling lingering effects, a report commissioned for the district showed.
That includes Marysville K-5 in the Foster-Powell neighborhood, which just four years ago received an influx of new students from Lent Elementary, which itself shifted to an all-school Spanish immersion model. All of Marysville’s students would make another shift, to Arleta K-5, if the closure and consolidation plans pass muster with the school board. Meanwhile, non-Spanish immersion students from Lent who were rezoned for Marysville just four years ago would move again, this time to Kelly K-5, which would now feed into Harrison Park Middle School instead of Brentwood Middle School.
Virtually any proposal the district put forward would have drawn a gale-force of criticism from some parents, some of whom are new to the district with children just starting out in elementary school. They’ve mobilized over the past few months to plead both for their own children’s schools and for the district to seek any possible alternative to closing schools that are at the beloved heart of local neighborhoods citywide.
They’ve contended that Portland Public Schools has moved too quickly, without specifying how much closing schools might save, what would become of vacant buildings, whether there would be layoffs of teachers and support staff and what evidence exists that combining schools would be good for students.
Matt Coolidge, the parent of a Rieke Elementary first grader and a preschooler, helped start a WhatsApp group for concerned parents that has since ballooned to include hundreds of families from around the city. The group is planning a rally before the school board meeting Tuesday.
“This is a huge shift, and let’s just make sure it’s a really well thought-out shift,” Coolidge said. “I just don’t think the evidence is there for that yet.”
The worst case scenario is if families are pitted against each other, arguing exclusively for their own schools, he said.
“That’s not going to be nearly as effective as a real show of force and unity among parents saying this is a community wide issue, a city issue,” Coolidge said.
Armstrong, who said she visited 17 schools in September to hear firsthand from community members, said she’d heard the concerns and skepticism, loud and clear.
“People don’t seem convinced that there’s an enrollment issue, and that there are schools that are not sustainable,” she said. “And they don’t believe that we’re doing everything we can to capture pupils in their neighborhood. They also don’t understand why we are rushing to close schools, in case kids come back.”
Enrollment forecasts from the Population Research Center at Portland State University predict that Portland Public Schools will lose another 5,100 students over the next decade — another three high school’s worth. Forecasters say that’s due to the declining birth rate and kindergarten capture rates that remain several percentage points below pre-pandemic levels as some families choose private, home-school or online school options.
The Portland Association of Teachers has pushed the district to grow enrollment and redraw attendance boundaries to redistribute existing students before closures are considered.
“The district can support social housing projects, reaching out to new families about the benefits of public education, and do more to publicize the great things happening in our schools,” PAT President Alisha Chavez wrote in a letter submitted to The Oregonian/OregonLive.
In practice, growing enrollment can be a painstaking, family-by-family effort, principals and PTA leaders who’ve worked on such efforts say, requiring sustained and personal interactions, like repeated visits to preschools, or hosting yearly events aimed at home school and private school families to showcase the academics, electives and sports available in the district’s middle and high schools.
The union has also zeroed in on the uncertainty surrounding what would become of vacated school buildings, posting on social media that the concept of “under-enrolled” schools is a constructed narrative that would open the door for private and charter schools to move into empty buildings.
“Decision of (this) magnitude take time and care, and PPS leadership has proven inept at making decisions of that scale in a rushed timeline,” Chavez said in a statement emailed to reporters after the district released its plans. “It’s critical that PPS get the process right, which requires slowing down and involving more stakeholders.”
Armstrong said she knows people are craving more information about what would happen to vacated buildings and said a team at the district is working on plans that may include using some campuses as swing sites while there’s seismic work or renovations taking place on others. Otherwise, she said her preference would be leasing empty buildings for childcare or early learning centers.
“We don’t need any more charters. We don’t need any more private schools,” she added.
Parents naturally want specifics about the kind of experience their children would have in consolidated and rezoned schools, but those details have been slow coming, Armstrong acknowledged.
Because most school funding in Oregon comes from the state, the school district has been reluctant to make promises without knowing who will win the upcoming gubernatorial election, or what the 2027 Legislature might decide about funding levels, she said.
“Our goal is to work at having full-time employees in the building, who are not moving from school to school,” she said. “Some of our teachers now move between three different schools in any given week. Our goal is absolutely class sizes that are in the mid-20s, to make sure that we have childcare and access to food banks, that there are safe walking routes to school and reliable, on time transportation.”
There’s somewhat limited research and reporting on the impact of school consolidations on students. Chicago’s huge experiment with closing more than 50 schools in 2013 didn’t solve the district’s massive budget issues or maintenance deficit, and enrollment declines have persisted. Meanwhile, Arkansas required the consolidation of all rural school districts with fewer than 350 students apiece; researchers from the University of Arkansas found the change had either “null or small positive impacts on student achievement in math and English Language Arts,” but “did not allow for a greater share of resources to be spent in the classroom.”
In Portland, district officials have pointed to the report they commissioned from Terrance Green, a University of Texas at Austin professor, and local education researcher Havala Hanson, who found that attendance zone shifts and program shuffles during changes in 2023 did not hurt student academic outcomes in Southeast Portland, though some schools reported significant disruptions to school culture. Further back, when the district closed the 750-student Marshall High School in 2011, students who went to either Franklin or McDaniel high schools instantly got access to a much broader set of courses and electives.
Even after Portland Public Schools navigates the bumpy waters ahead with the school closure decision, the district is still facing a $65 million budget deficit for the 2027-28 school year. Armstrong said she hopes it will be the final in a series of multimillion dollar cuts as the district works to better match its size and scope with its funding. The district has said repeatedly that closing schools is not a money-saving exercise, though inevitably, there would be some job losses, particularly among principals and office staff, who are assigned to every school no matter its size.
It’s not yet clear how many teachers might lose their jobs, given retirements that open up new positions each year, and because merged schools would need at least some additional teachers on staff.
The seven members of the school board all voted last spring to direct district staff to research closure scenarios, but that’s not the same as voting for a plan to close schools. Board members Christy Splitt and Stephanie Engelsman, in particular, have since expressed qualms about the scope and magnitude of the potential closures.
