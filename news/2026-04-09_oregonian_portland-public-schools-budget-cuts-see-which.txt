Title: Portland Public Schools budget cuts: See which schools lose the most staff

Author: Julia Silverman

High schools will bear the brunt of some $50 million in budget cuts planned for Portland Public Schools next year, according to new school-by-school breakdowns of staffing allocations provided to The Oregonian/OregonLive via a public records request.
The district has previously announced that it needed to shed about 300 jobs. Already, some principals have shared messages with families about which positions will be eliminated, shift to a reduced schedule or go unfilled in their individual schools. But a systemwide look at how hundreds fewer jobs will impact almost every school has not been available until now.
The new data shows that even high schools that are projected to gain students next year, including Franklin, Cleveland, Benson and Lincoln high schools, will have fewer overall staff. Each of those four schools will lose funding for between 2.5-4% of their staffs — despite being projected to gain between 20-60 students apiece.
The cuts go deepest at Roosevelt High School in North Portland, which is slated to lose just over 12 positions next year including at least 10 teachers. That equates to about 14% of the school’s staff. Enrollment at Roosevelt fell well below forecasts this school year — 109 students fewer, to be exact — and the district forecasts that enrollment will drop by another 87 students next year, which has led to the cutbacks.
Enrollment at Roosevelt, which is among the city’s most diverse high schools, will crater again in the 2027-2028 school year when several hundred more students will begin being funneled to North Portland’s Jefferson High School, and away from Roosevelt and the two other high schools in Northeast Portland, McDaniel and Grant. The change will result from new feeder patterns for middle schoolers. McDaniel High is also set to lose about 8% of its staff, amid the projected loss of nearly 60 students.
Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong and Chief Financial Officer Michelle Morrison have said that budget cuts are due to a complicated stew of factors. School districts across the state are coping with years of enrollment declines, and many have yet to reduce staff proportionately. They are also facing, higher costs for salaries, health care and retirement benefits, rising utility costs, the need to address escalating student behavioral and social-emotional needs and a distinct drop-off in local property tax revenues.
A spokesperson for Portland Public Schools said the numbers could still shift before the end of the year.
“School leaders have reviewed (these figures), and we are continuing to monitor enrollment closely and will adjust staffing as needed to ensure schools are appropriately supported,” said spokesperson Sydney Kelly.
Among other broad trends that have emerged from the school-by-school breakdowns:
- Almost no schools were held harmless, or are gaining staff hours. Among the few that are: Astor K-8 in North Portland, which will add middle school classroom educators, and Beverly Cleary K-8 in Northeast Portland, which is adding support staff to help with recess and lunch monitoring.
- The school district’s move to save money by blending classrooms in elementary school grades is visible nearly across the board. Kelly said it was not yet clear how many blended classrooms would exist systemwide, and how many would cover grades 1-2, as opposed to upper elementary grades.
- Funding earmarked for instructional coaches, a post-pandemic initiative to offer classroom teachers a steady stream of support and feedback in order to lift student learning, has been slashed. In its place: More flexibility for principals, who can now choose to use reduced funding to continue the coaching, or to pay for academic interventionists who work with students, specialists to help with behavior issues, social workers or school counselors.
- The district is sharply curtailing bridge funding that it had previously used to cushion the blow for schools no longer qualify for federal funding for extra staff to serve a certain threshold of high needs students.
No parent ever wants to hear about budget cuts at their children’s school, said Bonnie Lebenson, whose daughter is in kindergarten at Ainsworth Elementary in Southwest Portland, which is losing the equivalent of three staff positions, or about 9% of its staff, though enrollment is only projected to dip 3%.
She said she is particularly concerned about a lack of information around how special education needs will be supported, including for her own daughter, if and when early elementary grades are blended together, as well as whether cuts are disproportionately affecting high poverty and high needs populations.
“If you have one teacher across two grade levels and six have individualized education plans and there are going to be talented and gifted kids in there, and behavioral needs, the range of abilities is going to be huge,” Lebenson said. “How is a teacher doing this for the first time going to be able to manage this?”
Ainsworth Elementary is scheduled to host a community meeting Wednesday at which Lebenson said that she hopes to hear more specifics from building leaders and district officials.
Meanwhile, cuts are also continuing at the district’s central office. According to a memo that Armstrong sent to central office staff on Monday that was shared with The Oregonian/OregonLive, dozens of employees will find out next April 16 and April 17 that their jobs are being eliminated. In some cases, those cuts will be effective immediately; in others, Armstrong wrote, “employees may be asked to continue working for a period of time to support transition planning.”
“This is a heavy moment for many across our system,” Armstrong added in the memo. “With that, I want to be clear in mentioning that the decisions being made are not a reflection of individual performance or the value of any one person. They are driven by the financial realities we are working to address.”
