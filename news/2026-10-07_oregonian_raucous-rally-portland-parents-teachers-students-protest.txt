Title: At raucous rally, Portland parents, teachers and students protest school closures

Hundreds of infuriated parents and staff members descended on Portland Public Schools’ headquarters Tuesday night to protest far-reaching proposals to close up to 14 schools, shift grade configurations and programs and redraw attendance boundaries.
In numbers not seen since the Portland Association of Teachers strike, demonstrators chanted and cheered outside the district headquarters and scolded school board members during their meeting inside the building.
Some members of the crowd pressed inside the building even though the hearing room was at capacity.
“It’s insane to me how they can just uproot our community this way,” said Mariela Perez, who said she had graduated from James John K-5 and now sends her own children to the school, which could close if school board members choose to sign off on either of two closure proposals later this year. “The school is the heart of St. Johns. It is like taking a part of us away.”
Many — but not all — were there representing the 14 schools named for potential closures. Others criticized the district’s plans to phase out almost all of its K-8 middle schools.
“This whole process feels really unfair to families,” said Caitlin Burkhart, whose children attend Rose City Park K-5 in Northeast Portland, which is slated for closure under one of the proposed scenarios.
Seeing Rose City Park on the list was a shock, Burkhart said, because the school had been set to get a full seismic retrofit, a multimillion-dollar investment that many in the community had assumed would keep it open.
Under one of the two proposals, Rose City Park’s neighborhood students would go to Scott Elementary; students in its Vietnamese immersion program would transfer to Vestal K-5.
“The suggestion that they would split our school in half feels antithetical,” Burkhart said. “We are one school, not two.”
It was the first chance for public feedback since the district’s proposals were unveiled Monday evening. Virtually everyone in attendance was there to urge school board members to hit pause on the proposed plans until there was time for more feedback.
Some pleaded for their own children’s schools or questioned why their schools were chosen while others were left untouched; others lamented that the situation had left schools pitted against each other.
Alisha Chavez, the president of the Portland Association of Teachers, which has emerged as a key adversary of the district’s plans, said district leaders have failed to provide enough specifics about their proposals to earn the public’s trust.
“You have to be able to tell the community what their children would receive in return and we haven’t heard that,” Chavez said. “When you close a school, you don’t just move children from one building to another. You move them away from their people. We are not rightsizing education, we are reducing it.”

Though the crowd reserved most of their animus for district leadership, Gov. Tina Kotek and state lawmakers did not escape unscathed.
“The leadership (Kotek) could provide to prevent this is so missing and so noticeable,” said Jennifer Starkey, who works at an environmental nonprofit that partners with the school district. “There is a degree of accountability that we deserve.”
Some parents said they felt more grief than fury, mixed with acceptance.
“We are sad and disappointed about our school building closing,” said Haley Hatfield, who has two students at Irvington K-5, which would close under both proposals. The school’s students would move to Beverly Cleary K-5, and then on to Beaumont Middle School, instead of their former assignment to Harriet Tubman Middle School.
“We are worried about our staff, our principal, which beloved teachers we might lose,” Hatfield added. “But the fact that we all got to stay together has been preventing people from wanting to fight it.”
The conversation about consolidating schools, adjusting boundaries and reconfiguring grades is primarily driven by enrollment declines and rising operational costs that have left resources stretched perennially thin across many of the district’s schools, even as deferred maintenance backlogs have piled up.
For at least the past eight years, the district has been shifting money from high schools to subsidize elementary schools, Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong said Tuesday night.
As schools have gotten smaller — about half of the district’s elementary schools have fewer than 300 students and a handful have only half that number — it gets harder to afford stable staffing, with multiple homerooms at each grade level and specialists who are assigned to single schools, said Margaret Calvert, the district’s executive director of school planning and modernization.
In her testimony to board members Tuesday, Chavez acknowledged the realities of declining enrollment — largely, but not entirely due to a decline in birthrates — and tight budgets, including the $65 million deficit on the horizon for the 2027-28 school year.
But she said the solution was not to shrink schools, but to invest in them, adding “more programs, more supports, smaller class sizes, to create schools that families desperately want to choose.”
