Title: Plan to redraw Portland high school boundaries moves forward in emotional vote
Outlet: The Oregonian/OregonLive
Author: Julia Silverman
Date: 2026-01-14

Note: Fetched via browser (curl blocked with 403).

Portland Public School Board members signed off Tuesday on a sweeping plan to revamp high school attendance zones across North and Northeast Portland, starting in fall 2027.
The change will bolster enrollment at North Portland’s Jefferson High School, and sunset a 15-year-old policy that allowed families to choose between sending their children to Jefferson or one of three other high schools in the quadrant, depending on their address.
Instead, board members voted 6-1 to send almost all children who are zoned for Harriet Tubman and Ockley Green middle schools and Vernon K-8 and Faubion PK-8 on to Jefferson. That would cover elementary students at Beach, Chief Joseph, Boise-Eliot/Humboldt, Dr. Martin Luther King, Jr., Sabin and Woodlawn schools.
The only exceptions would be for students who began school at either Peninsula Elementary or as part of Beach Elementary’s dual language Spanish immersion program, who would go to Roosevelt High, and students who had attended Irvington Elementary, who would be zoned solely for Grant High.
The ability to choose between Jefferson and either Grant, Roosevelt or McDaniel high schools, depending on a family’s address, had hollowed out the student population at Jefferson, a historically Black high school in Portland’s Albina neighborhood. This year, the school enrolls fewer than 400 students, while the three other schools have between 1,400 and 2,200 students apiece.
Jefferson has a robust partnership with Portland Community College, allowing its students to earn college credit, but currently offers far fewer advanced courses and career/technical education electives than the city’s seven comprehensive high schools.
To convince parents who are skeptical about transitioning their students to Jefferson, Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong has promised that offerings at the school will eventually equal or surpass those at other high schools citywide.
The resolution approved by the school board calls for the district to fund two extra staff positions at Jefferson through the 2030-2031 school year to ease the transition. Filip Hristić, the district’s senior director of academics for grades 6-12, said those two educators will be used to “expand academic programming,” roles that will become clearer as students headed to Jefferson weigh in on the courses and electives they hope to take there.
The changes come as Jefferson is slated for a $465 million remodel. Construction is set to begin this spring. Students will remain on campus in the old building while a new one is constructed; it’s expected to be fully ready for students and staff by the start of the 2029-2030 school year. All construction is expected to be complete by 2030, unless the district can find a way to speed up that schedule, perhaps by moving students into the first two floors of the new building while construction continues on upper levels.
Fallout from the change will be felt at Grant, Roosevelt and McDaniel high schools. The district has said its goal is to maintain at least 1,100 students at each of those three schools, plus Jefferson, in order to offer a broad array of courses and field competitive sports teams. But it’s unclear that there are enough students in the pipeline to meet that threshold at all four schools, amid cratering birth rates and with Portland’s perennially high housing prices a deterrent for younger families.
The rezoning is projected to reduce enrollment at the three other high schools in the North and Northeast quadrant by about 150 students at McDaniel High School in the Madison South neighborhood, 300 students at Roosevelt High in the St. Johns neighborhood and at least 700 students at Grant High School in the Grant Park neighborhood.
Board members nodded to that upheaval Tuesday by including a clause in their resolution requiring the district to monitor changes in enrollment and staffing, plus impacts on athletics, extracurriculars and course offerings at all four high schools, over the next five years and notify families about their findings.
Projections by The Oregonian/OregonLive show that Roosevelt is most likely to fall well below 1,100 students by the 2031-2032 school year. Ian Ritorto, the student representative to the school board and a Roosevelt student, wondered openly at Tuesday’s board meeting whether students who live across the Willamette River in the Linnton and Forest Park neighborhoods who currently are zoned for Lincoln High School might eventually be rezoned for Roosevelt to balance the numbers.
Stephanie Engelsman, the only board member to vote against approving the attendance boundaries, said Thursday that she had done so because she would have “preferred an alternative proposal that strengthened all of our high schools while also providing Jefferson with the enrollment to function and thrive as a comprehensive high school.”
Engelsman said she was concerned about the impact of enrollment reductions on Roosevelt in particular, which serve a large number of historically underserved students, as well as at Grant High School.
Originally, the district presented a handful of potential scenarios for the redistricting, all of which had their champions and their critics among parents who followed the conversation closely.
For example, a contingent of families from Vernon and Faubion had pushed hard for students from Irvington Elementary to also be zoned for Jefferson, arguing that exempting those families imperiled Jefferson’s enrollment numbers, and thus its ability to field a wide range of academics, electives and athletics.
In a letter to board members, Vernon parent Bethany Gumper wrote that the scenario approved Tuesday would “continue a long-standing pattern of concentrating students and wealth at Grant … at the expense of Jefferson.”
Some parents from Sabin Elementary, meanwhile, had lobbied for their school to remain a Grant feeder.
“You don’t build up one school by tearing down another. You don’t help one community by dismantling a different community. Jefferson should rise because it is a great school that families want to attend, not because you are giving some families no other choice,” Sabin parent Amy Garlock wrote to board members.
Other parents, particularly those of current 6th and 7th graders who will spend their first year or two of high school in a school under construction that is still building up its academic offerings, said they wanted more certainty from the district.
“It’s one thing to be on campus during construction,” parent Trinity Toft wrote to the board. “It’s another to not have access to classes and activities that your peers at other schools have. [Portland Public Schools] should be looking for every opportunity to ensure that cohort has a full high school experience from day one. Otherwise, we’ll be extending a legacy of disinvestment in the Jefferson community, and that is unacceptable.”
On Tuesday, Armstrong and several board members said they were acutely aware of the district’s long and sometimes painful history with Jefferson, and said their vote marked a new, more hopeful and long overdue direction for the school and its neighborhood.
“This has been a generational journey to this point,” said Margaret Calvert, a former Jefferson principal who is now an assistant superintendent under Armstrong. “This resolution will lead to the next chapter in the century-long, rich and storied history of Jefferson High School … reinvigorating Jefferson as the vibrant learning center in the heart of Albina.”
