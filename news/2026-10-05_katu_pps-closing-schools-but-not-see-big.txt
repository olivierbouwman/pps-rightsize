PPS is closing schools but may not see a big budget payoff. Here's why.
PORTLAND, Ore. (KATU) — When the school bell rings for the new school year in the fall of 2027, hundreds of Portland Public Schools (PPS) elementary and middle school students may find themselves at a new school.
The district’s rightsizing effort is intended to reallocate resources to better serve an evolving, and dropping, student population.
PPS has consistently said the process is focused on student outcomes, not dollars and cents. But a KATU investigation found school closures may not deliver a major financial windfall for the district.
“The budget savings that usually happen in these right-sizing processes are not very large,” said Dr. Gabriel Huddleston, director for the Center for Public Education and Community Engagement at Texas Christian University. “You're not talking about a huge amount of money that's going to move the needle one way or the other.”

Huddleston said changing demographics and declining urban populations have put school systems across the country in a similar position.
“We just have a much more mobile kind of student population than we ever had before and this combined with kind of declining populations in urban centers have led to schools struggling with declining enrollments,” Huddleston said.
Huddleston’s research found school systems across the country have closed hundreds of schools in the last decade. The research shows savings can be minimal, mainly because districts do not necessarily cut staff at closed schools. Instead, they may move those employees to schools with more students after rightsizing.
“Like let's say ideally Miss Smith has this new class. Well, Miss Jones or Mr. Jones who is teaching over at the other school that got closed, we're going to move him over, and he's going to teach another section of fourth grade,” Huddleston said. “Well, you haven't saved any money because you're still employing the same amount of teachers. You're just moving them around into different schools.”
What could happen with vacant properties
The biggest possible financial impact could come from offloading vacant properties to a new owner.
The school board chair and district declined to talk specifically about that. However, the district’s policy for excess property shows the public should not expect a fire sale of empty schools.

The board’s policy outlines what should happen with excess property. The goal is to keep the property in public hands.
A sale is a last-case scenario and comes only after a lengthy public process. Even then, the policy says, “any District property sale should be to a public entity that intends to actively use the property for public purposes[.]”
In other words, the policy disfavors selling to a developer who would bulldoze a school and build housing.
It is not clear whether any public body in Portland has the budget to buy property, given budget restrictions at all levels of government.
To the contrary, a dozen or more newly vacant schools could come with their own cost. Take Smith School in Southwest Portland, for example. Records KATU obtained through a public-records request show the district spent roughly $70,000 there in one year. That covered utilities, security, work orders and maintenance.
What experts say districts should do
Huddleston said there is plenty districts can do to get rightsizing right. That includes creating a clear plan for where students will go and how they will be supported. He said districts should prioritize equity, keep strong teachers, and involve families and communities from the start.
He said communication is key, and districts actually can’t over communicate during the process.
“You want to engage the stakeholders from the beginning,” Huddleston said. “You don't want to make this seem like this is something coming out of nowhere. You want to engage the community, the parents, neighbors, all of those different students, teachers. And you want to get them involved in the process so they feel like they have a voice like if we're going do this, at least I feel like I've been heard.”
Huddleston said residents should also participate, recommending they email principals, district leaders, the superintendent and school board members to make their views known.
