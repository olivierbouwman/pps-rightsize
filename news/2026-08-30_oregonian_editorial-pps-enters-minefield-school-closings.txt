Title: Editorial: PPS enters the minefield of school closings
Outlet: The Oregonian/OregonLive
Author: Editorial Board
Date: 2026-08-30

No one willingly enters a minefield unless there’s no other way to reach safer ground. Portland families should remember that as Portland Public Schools Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong navigates the explosive terrain of school closures.
This isn’t where anyone wants to be. But PPS has been losing students for years, with enrollment expected to decline another 15% by 2039, as The Oregonian/OregonLive’s Julia Silverman reported.
Many schools across the district have far too few students to afford the staff and services that students in each building need. Schools have combined fourth and fifth graders into single classrooms and spread librarians, art and gym teachers across multiple schools. At the same time, PPS’ costs continue to soar, forcing layoffs and other cuts for four years running — with another $65 million deficit projected for the year ahead.
Consolidating students into fewer buildings won’t begin to solve the district’s budget problems. But it will help stabilize the district and ensure there are more adults in the remaining schools to deliver a full-service, quality education. While the district must also consider the needs of educators, families and others, consolidation is about preserving the educational experience for children who depend on schools to give them the skills, opportunities and support that will help them thrive.
That must be the compass that leads the community from start to finish through what is going to be a very difficult and emotional process.
The district has a lot of work to do over the next few months. As Silverman reported, PPS is looking at closing up to 20 schools, and is evaluating enrollment patterns, building conditions, transportation services and many other needs. Armstrong is expected to share some details in early September of how many schools the district will aim to close or redraw attendance boundaries, with a list of possible school closure scenarios in October for the public and school board to weigh.
Understandably, some in the community are already girding for battle. The Portland Association of Teachers has launched a petition calling for redrawing boundaries to even out student populations; investing in teachers and in-classroom staff versus outside contracts; and selling the PPS headquarters while relocating central office staff to work out of school buildings instead.
Others, in social media posts, are calling for better funding, rallying their neighborhood communities and raising suspicions that closures will hit the poorest communities the hardest. It’s a hint of just how fierce the conversation will turn once actual school names are on the table.
That makes it all the more important for the district to go overboard in communicating with the public each step of the way. That means laying out the tentative timeline; explaining the values and goals driving proposals and decisions; providing the data underpinning proposals; articulating the considerations and tradeoffs of each proposal; and alerting families to opportunities for learning more and providing feedback, both-in-person and through email or other electronic means. In Salem-Keizer Public Schools, where the district is also embarking on school closures, Superintendent Andrea Casteneda has recorded a series of videos that provide helpful information to families, promoting both clarity and transparency.

PPS should also show how these changes will be made with an eye to the future with plans to more broadly offer the popular programs and opportunities that families want. While consolidation is an act of survival, PPS and its partners must also design for a district that encourages families to return and demonstrates a commitment to excellence that makes public education the easy choice. Just over a decade ago, 82% of families living in PPS boundaries sent their children to PPS schools. That has since fallen to 74%, and with birth rates declining and less migration into the city, the overall number continues to shrink.
Community leaders, elected or not, should commit to playing a constructive role in this process. State and local officials can help identify potential solutions for problems that may arise due to consolidation, such as unmet transportation needs or similar gaps. They can also make better progress on easing Portland’s affordability crisis and boosting its reputation as a great place to raise a family. But at the very least, they should refrain from the self-serving dogpiling that Portland-area legislators engaged in during the 2023 teachers strike, when they blasted the district for not doing more to meet teachers’ demands — even though legislators had not provided the funding to do so.
And Portland families have a responsibility to do their own fact-gathering rather than rely on social media or blindly trusting the assertions of one side or another. Look for those addressing questions with factual information rather than with inflammatory language. They should also check their assumptions of what changes could mean: A larger school, for instance, does not necessarily mean larger class sizes, because the number of students could then justify adding another teacher.
In the coming months, there will be complex tradeoffs, conflicting priorities and many who will feel crushed by the final decision, even if it’s ultimately best for the community as a whole. At the same time, there will be a lot of accusations, misinformation and appeals to emotion, mistrust and suspicion.
No one wants any of this, but this is the situation we are in. Any honest evaluation of the facts must recognize that there’s no realistic alternative or pipeline of cash coming to rescue the district.
Armstrong’s leadership isn’t beyond reproach, and it’s fair to disagree with her decisions. But Portlanders should recognize her courage in proceeding down this path. As a community with the shared goal of preserving the best educational opportunities for students, let’s help disarm the mines, not detonate them.
-The Oregonian/OregonLive Editorial Board
