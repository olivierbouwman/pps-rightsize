Title: Fury over eliminating high school grade levels at one PPS school foreshadows tough conversations on districtwide closures

Students, parents and teachers excoriated Portland Public Schools leaders this week over the abrupt plan to eliminate the high school grade levels at Metropolitan Learning Center in Northwest Portland, a preview of the highly charged dynamics around school closures awaiting Oregon’s largest district in the next 18 months.
At a Wednesday night listening session at the K-12 school’s auditorium, Superintendent Kimberlee Armstrong offered a mea culpa for announcing the decision to close the 40-student high school at the end of this academic year without a prior chance for any community feedback.
“We could have done better, and we recognize that,” Armstrong said. “We could have taken the time to do a process that was more inclusive and informative and interactive with the community.”
But, Armstrong added, the district also has a responsibility to make tough decisions in the best interest of the entire school system and to be upfront about those choices.
“Do we say to the community, ‘Here’s a decision we need to make, and I want to hear from you, and let’s spend three to four months to share the information and getting feedback for us to arrive at where we are going to arrive at anyway?” she asked. “I feel like we should be honest and transparent about where we are at.”
She also acknowledged that the decision to shift the alternative school to a K-8 model was under legal review, since it was not vetted by the full school board before being announced, a possible conflict with board policy on school closures. That policy centers on the shutdown of an entire school, not a shift in grade levels offered. Teachers at MLC have contended that the high school has its own identification number within Synergy, the district’s student management system, making it a school in its own right.
School board members will take up the issue during next Tuesday’s school board meeting.
Armstrong also said that the handling of the MLC decision, and the resulting furor, will inform the districtwide conversations about closures that are expected to begin in earnest this spring. She hinted that the district could ultimately recommend closing or combining a dozen or more schools by fall 2027.
“We are going to have many conversations over the next 12 to 18 months about closing multiple campuses across Portland Public Schools,” Armstrong said. “The reality is we have 81 different sites. And even after this decision, we’ll still have 81 sites. We need to have a conversation about getting that 81 number down to be in the 70s or even further with the declining enrollment that we have going on.”
The school district, Oregon’s largest, has about 5,900 fewer students than it did before the pandemic — a 13% decline in enrollment since the fall of 2019 — but the same number of schools. Around two dozen of its elementary schools enroll fewer than 300 students.
At Wednesday’s meeting, which drew about 150 people, students, staff, parents and alumni of Metropolitan Learning Center spoke emotionally about the school, calling it a safe haven for students who felt unwelcome or unsafe at traditional neighborhood high schools.
“MLC really serves a purpose,” said Sharon Forrest, a special education teacher at the school. “Kids find their place here. They find their people here. They find relationships with teachers who know them by their first names and work with them. It is disheartening and disrespectful that we would just find this out a few weeks ago without any input from teachers and the community.”
Armstrong said the decision to close the high school at MLC was tied to the $50 million budget deficit that the district faces and not a reflection on the school’s value to the community.
“It’s a conversation of where are the numbers low and where are the costs to educate our students higher than at other places,” along with whether there are other campuses within the district that can offer similar education experiences, she said.
Chief of Schools Isaac Cardona told families that there were a number of other non-traditional high school options, including the newly-renovated Alliance High School, which is designed for at-risk students seeking an alternative to a large comprehensive high school, the career and technical focused Benson High School, and Jefferson High School, which has a partnership with Portland Community College.
The school district also contracts with community-based organizations that run smaller high school programs for students with complex needs, like the Mount Scott Learning Center or NAYA’s Many Nations Academy, Cardona said.
But audience members said they were skeptical that any of the options would be the right fit for MLC’s population. The school does not exclusively serve neurodiverse students, but many of its high schoolers identify as such.
“Our student body gets othered and made fun of a lot for our unique differences,” said Nolan Cerny, a ninth grader at MLC. “But this place, when I got here, it made me feel at home. I don’t think I would be the same person that I am today without this school. I’ve never been to a traditional school, and I’m nervous about how me and my peers will transition.”
Others in the audience, including State Sen. Lew Frederick, a Portland Democrat who used to teach at MLC, warned that dissolving the high school was just the first step toward a complete building closure.
“To cut off a piece doesn’t leave the rest of school alive,” said Ned Hascall, who teaches art at the school. “We might save the salaries of four and a half teachers, but when it’s gone, it’s gone, and there is no way to get it back.”
Armstrong, though, said the district sees a need for more alternative options at the middle school level, and could seek to expand available spots at MLC for grades 6-8 after the high school changeover is complete. Entrance to the school, as at other neighborhood school alternatives, is managed via a lottery system.
