# Keep Sunnyside Environmental K-8 As It Is

**The ask:** remove the Sunnyside changes from the rightsizing recommendation. That means keeping Sunnyside a K-8, keeping its environmental focus option, and keeping its neighborhood-plus-lottery model.

Sunnyside is not being closed, so this change does not close a building or save a lease. It ends one of PPS's few working programs to solve a problem the program didn't create.

## The case in seven points

1. **It works by the district's own measures.** Rightsizing exists to fix under-enrolled, unstable schools. Sunnyside is neither.

   | Measure | Sunnyside | District | Sunnyside's rank |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Building full (2025-26) | **89%** (485 / 546 seats) | 68% overall; median school 66% | **4th of 67** neighborhood schools below high school; 7th of 72 including focus schools; top 15% of all 82 |
   | Enrollment change, last two years | **+8.5%** (447 → 485) | −2.9% | **9th of 86** schools; only 25 grew at all |
   | Forecast change, 2025-26 → 2035-36 (PSU) | **−1.9%** (485 → 476) | −13.6%; median school −10.8% | **12th of 80**; one of only 18 schools projected to lose 5% or less |
   | Neighborhood capture rate (Oct 2025) | **77%** of neighborhood students choose it | median 72% | Above median (23rd of 77) |

2. **The district couldn't say why Sunnyside.** Director Splitt asked on Oct 6 why Sunnyside was picked. Staff answered: *"Sunnyside just happened to be one of the ones just arbitrarily because some of them needed to go and some needed to stay."* A program families have relied on since the 1990s shouldn't end by coin flip.

3. **It would close a focus option while the district says it closes none.** The scenario memo states "No alternative or focus option school closure is proposed." Yet the same memo "discontinues" Sunnyside's focus option, ends its lottery and removes its grades 6–8 from a school that stays open. That is a program closure in everything but name.

4. **It serves the students rightsizing says it protects.** 23.5% of Sunnyside students receive special education (114 students). That is above the median K-5/K-8 school (19.0%) and well above its neighbors:

   | School | Special education share |
   |---|---|
   | Sunnyside | 23.5% |
   | Hosford | 15.7% |
   | Laurelhurst | 15.1% |
   | Abernethy | 14.5% |
   | Winterhaven | 7.0% |

   Parents testified that Sunnyside's outdoor, field-study model is why their neurodivergent children can learn at all. The research is consistent with that, within limits. Systematic reviews find outdoor learning's most reliable benefits are engagement, behavior and well-being, with promising evidence for students with ADHD and other disabilities; test-score effects are mixed (Mann et al. 2022; Becker et al. 2017; Szczytko et al. 2018). Studies of school moves also find that students from smaller settings, and students with learning disabilities placed in larger grade cohorts, are hurt most (Beuchert et al. 2018; Gershenson & Langbein 2015). Moving 193 middle-schoolers from a school where nearly a quarter of students have IEPs into a larger middle school is a real risk to exactly these students.

5. **The rules aren't applied the same way.** Winterhaven, a K-8 focus option with similar poverty levels (18% vs. Sunnyside's 19%), keeps its middle grades in both scenarios at 316 students. That is far below the district's own 500-student K-8 minimum. Sunnyside, at 485, loses its middle grades. We're not asking for anything taken from Winterhaven, only for the same standard.

6. **It already is the model the board says it wants.**
   - Resolution 7307's "Educational Options" principle says "neighborhood schools are the foundation of our school system" and that access to choice programs "will be balanced with the goal of assuring well-resourced neighborhood schools." Sunnyside already meets that test: it is a full, stable neighborhood school (219 neighborhood students, 77% of the area's children choose it) that also hosts a choice program (45% of students from the neighborhood, 55% by lottery). Ending the program doesn't strengthen its neighborhood school. It removes more than half the students and backfills with Buckman's.
   - Board members praised exactly this model in the spring:
     - Sullivan (Apr 27, probable attribution): having "the neighborhood school and the focus together… sounds like… a win-win. I know Sunnyside does it as well."
     - Splitt, the same night: "neighborhood schools plus focus option is such a cool idea."
     - Chase-Miller (Apr 28, probable): "really interested in the idea of focus option schools that exist in neighborhood schools… we're also not pulling kids out of their neighborhood."
     - Chair Wang (Apr 28, probable): he wants "not a closure solution but potentially maybe… how the lottery is done or a policy solution."
   - Sunnyside is the model they described. Ending it contradicts what four directors said they wanted.
   - It is also PPS's only environmental K-8. It puts the board's own Climate Crisis Response, Climate Justice and Sustainable Practices policy into daily practice.

7. **It protects students at the exact point PPS loses them.** The district's own Successful Schools Survey (Mar 2026) shows a cliff at the move into middle grades:

   | Measure | Grade 5 | Grade 6 | Grade 8 |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Sense of belonging | 64% | 49% | 37% |
   | Engagement | 51% | 34% | 20% |

   PPS's grade 6–12 belonging score sits at the **10th national percentile**. Director La Forte, seeing this, said she'd "always believed that middle schools were [better], but the more recent research has actually shown the opposite." She asked staff to split the results by K-8 versus middle school; that split has not been published. Sunnyside's K-8 keeps students in one community through that transition.

   **The peer-reviewed research points the same way.** The strongest studies compare students who stayed in K-8s with those who moved to middle school in grade 6:

   | Study | Effect of moving to middle school in grade 6 |
   |---|---|
   | New York City (Rockoff & Lockwood 2010) | Math −0.18 SD, English −0.16 SD in the first year; still about 0.17 / 0.14 SD behind K-8 peers by grade 8 |
   | Florida statewide (Schwerdt & West 2013) | Math −0.12 SD, reading −0.09 SD; gap grows to 0.23 / 0.14 SD by grade 8; early dropout up about 18%; **no payoff in high school** |
   | North Carolina (Cook et al. 2008) | Substantially more discipline infractions, persisting into grade 9 |

   - Losses were larger for low-achieving students.
   - These studies don't break out students with disabilities.
   - The NYC and Florida effect sizes come from the authors' published summaries.

   Removing a working K-8 should at least wait for the K-8-versus-middle data La Forte requested.

**And it's cheaper than most.** Sunnyside spends $10,462 per student. That is below the median PPS school ($11,450) and the median K-8 ($10,989), and it is the **21st least expensive of 81** schools. No savings figure for this change has been published.

## Answering the district's reasons

| Their reason | Our answer |
|---|---|
| *Hosford needs the students.* It is forecast to drop from 528 to about 370–390, below the 500 middle-school minimum. | Only 82 of Sunnyside's 193 middle-schoolers live in the Sunnyside area. The rest would go back to many different middle schools, so Hosford gains roughly 80–110 students, not 193. Hosford is also receiving Llewellyn-area students from closing Sellwood. **Ask the district to publish Hosford's 2031-32 projection with and without Sunnyside's 6–8.** If Sellwood alone gets Hosford past 500, the Sunnyside change isn't needed. |
| *Focus options pull students from neighborhood schools.* | Sunnyside draws modestly from any one school: 31 students from the Hosford area, 16 from Glencoe, 15 from Abernethy. If that's the concern, cap lottery seats from under-enrolled schools, an idea Director Splitt has raised. Don't end the program. |
| *Middle schools outperform K-8s.* | Sunnyside's results are on par with Hosford's: math 53% vs. 51%, reading/language 61% vs. 66%. That comes with nearly 8 percentage points more students in special education. Belonging and engagement collapse in PPS after 5th grade (see point 7), and La Forte has said recent research favors K-8s. Peer-reviewed studies from NYC and Florida find the opposite: moving to middle school in grade 6 lowers achievement, and Florida found no high-school payoff from middle schools (see point 7). Ask for the K-8-vs-middle split of the survey and Sunnyside-specific 9th-grade outcomes. |
| *Sunnyside is below the 500-student K-8 minimum.* | The 360 and 500 thresholds come from the state's Quality Education Model, a *funding prototype* that also assumes about 20 students per class (PPS's Aug 4 memo; the SEGC evaluation). No research sets a minimum school size. The best reviews suggest *maximums* of about 300–500 for elementary-age students, especially disadvantaged ones (Leithwood & Jantzi 2009). At 485 students and 89% full, Sunnyside is within 3% of the prototype. Meanwhile the plan would push Hosford well above it. |
| *Focus options increase segregation in the schools left behind.* | That's a real finding in the research (Saporito 2003), and Sunnyside isn't immune. Its share of historically underserved students (17.7%) is below the district's. The fix is the fallback ask: lottery equity weighting (priority for students from Title I and under-enrolled areas) and caps on draws from struggling schools. That makes the program more equitable without ending it. Sunnyside's 19% poverty rate matches Winterhaven's, which keeps its program. |
| *Sunnyside lost enrollment over the decade.* | True: 602 → 485 since 2016-17 (−19%), while the district fell 12%. But the trend has reversed. Sunnyside grew 8.5% in two years while the district kept shrinking, and PSU now forecasts it flat through 2035. That is the profile of a school that's stabilized, not one that needs restructuring. |
| *Changes "cascade."* | Keeping Sunnyside is the smallest possible change. Nothing closes, nothing new opens. The only knock-on effects are a modest share of Buckman's area and Hosford's numbers, and both can be modeled. |

## What we're asking for, in order

1. **Keep Sunnyside as is** (K-8, environmental focus, hybrid lottery), and remove it from the Nov 17 recommendation.
2. **If not, keep the K-8 and the environmental program** with lottery limits: neighborhood preference, and caps on seats from under-enrolled schools.
3. **At minimum:**
   - Keep the environmental program for neighborhood K-5 students, as Splitt suggested.
   - Add lottery equity weighting (priority for students from Title I and under-enrolled areas) instead of ending the lottery.
   - Phase grades 6–8 out only as current students finish.
   - Publish a Sunnyside-specific fiscal and enrollment analysis before the vote.
   - Publish the Successful Schools Survey split by K-8 versus middle school, which La Forte requested on Mar 31.

## How to sell it

**Timing matters most. The best moment is before Nov 17.** Chair Wang has said changes after the recommendation "cascade" and must go back through staff. Winning with staff now is far easier than winning an amendment in December.

**To PPS staff and the superintendent** (engagement sessions and the survey through late October):
- Use the district's own language: well-resourced, sustainable, stable enrollment, the Educational Options principle, equity for special-education students.
- Ask for three specific things in writing:
    1. Hosford's projection with and without Sunnyside 6–8.
    2. The savings from this change.
    3. Why Sunnyside rather than another focus option.
- Be polite and data-first. The "arbitrary" answer is the opening; don't gloat about it.

**To the board** (detail in [`SUNNYSIDE_BOARD_GUIDE.md`](SUNNYSIDE_BOARD_GUIDE.md)):
- **Four votes are needed, and four directors are on record for this model or this question.**
    - Engelsman is the natural lead; Sunnyside is in her zone.
    - Sullivan called the neighborhood-plus-focus model "a win-win" and named Sunnyside.
    - Splitt called it "such a cool idea" and asked "why Sunnyside?"
    - La Forte asked "why Sunnyside?" and says research now favors K-8s.
    - Chase-Miller and Wang are reachable by proposing to *fix the lottery instead of closing the program*. That matches Wang's April 28 comment that he wants "not a closure solution" but a lottery or "policy solution."
- **Frame it as subtraction.** Removing a change fits Splitt's "scalpel" and her "too much change" concern. Ask that it be removed, not that anything else be added.
- **Send each director one short email** with one data point aimed at what they care about, not a mass campaign.
- **Quote directors back to themselves, respectfully.** Their own spring statements are the strongest material we have.
- **Get directors to put the three questions on the board question tracker.** Then the answers become public record.
- **Speak at the Oct 13 Teaching, Learning & Enrollment committee meeting** on immersion and middle grades. La Forte chairs it; Splitt and Chase-Miller sit on it.

**To the media:**
- **The hook:** *"PPS says it isn't closing any focus schools. It's closing one, and staff called the choice 'arbitrary.'"* That's a clean, quotable story with the district's own words.
- **Secondary angles:**
  - One of the fullest, fastest-growing schools in PPS being dismantled during a shrinking-enrollment crisis. It is 89% full against a 68% district average, with +8.5% growth in two years against −2.9% district-wide. Only three neighborhood schools below high school are fuller.
  - Special-education students losing the setting that works for them.
  - PPS's only environmental school ending during its own climate-policy push.
- **Spokespeople:**
  - A parent of a student with an IEP, speaking for their own family only.
  - A long-time teacher.
  - A Sunnyside middle-schooler, with parental consent, describing field studies.
  - **City Councilor Tiffany Koyama-Lane**, a former Sunnyside teacher, as a possible validator if she's willing.
- **Visuals:** students outdoors on a Thursday field study, and the simple enrollment chart (447 → 461 → 485, flat forecast).
- **The precedent to cite: MLC.** In February PPS abruptly moved to end the Metropolitan Learning Center's high school grades, in a K-12 alternative school that stays open. That is the same shape as the Sunnyside change.
  - Coverage was sympathetic. OPB later reported that "staff and students rallied to keep it open" and PPS "committed to boosting enrollment there."
  - Both scenarios now target MLC's high school again, so its community is a natural ally. Pitch the two together as a pattern: *PPS keeps cutting the grade levels of alternative programs that work.*

- **Opinion pages:**
  - **Oregonian "Readers respond":** it has run eight PPS letters since 2025, including one urging PPS to keep MLC's high school. Send short letters from different voices: a parent, a teacher, an alum.
  - **An op-ed** from a credible validator (a former teacher, Koyama-Lane, or an environmental-education leader).
  - **Editorial boards:** both the Oregonian and WW endorsed La Forte and Splitt in 2025. Their editorial voice carries weight with those two directors.
- **Tone match:**
  - Directors on all sides read the Oregonian.
  - Some community advocates have publicly criticized the Oregonian editorial board's and WW's framing (Dec 2025 public comment on the Center for Black Student Excellence).
  - For Chase-Miller, an OPB or TV story centered on special-education students will land better than a WW accountability piece.
- **Do:**
  - Stay positive ("keep what works").
  - Acknowledge that PPS faces real cuts.
  - Stand with other affected communities.
- **Don't:**
  - Attack Winterhaven, Buckman or Title I schools. That reads as privilege and loses Wang, DePass and Chase-Miller.
  - Call for pausing all of rightsizing. That puts us with the opposition the majority has already dismissed.
  - Overclaim test scores.
