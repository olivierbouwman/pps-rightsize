# What the Research Says About School Closures and Rightsizing

As of 2026-10-07. A summary of **145 studies and reports (112 peer-reviewed)** on school closures, consolidation, grade configuration, school size, costs and process, from the US and abroad. Each item has a one-page summary in [`research-literature/`](research-literature/), and each of the three topic folders has its own index and synthesis.

**How to read the evidence.**
- A **meta-analysis** statistically pools results across many studies. A **systematic review** collects all studies on a question by explicit rules and appraises them. Both are stronger than any single study.
- **There is no formal meta-analysis of school-closure effects.** The best syntheses are narrative reviews, chiefly Tieken & Auldridge-Reveles (2019, *Review of Educational Research*).
- The strongest single studies are **quasi-experimental**: they follow the same students before and after a closure and compare them with similar students elsewhere.
- Most US causal evidence comes from closing **low-performing** schools. PPS's closures are **enrollment-driven**, a less-studied case.

---

## Bottom line

1. **Closures usually do little for displaced students' test scores on average, and impose a real short-term disruption.**
   - The disruption often starts in the announcement year. Chicago students lost about 1.5 months of reading that year.
   - Students gain only when they move to a clearly better school. When adequate neighborhood schools close, as in PPS's case, that upside is smallest.
2. **The receiving school decides the outcome.**
   - Results for students already in receiving schools range from no harm (Pittsburgh) to harm that grows with the share of newcomers and with travel distance (Philadelphia).
   - Concentrating displaced students and lengthening commutes predict worse results.
3. **Converting K-8s to K-5 + middle school carries measurable risk.**
   - In NYC and Florida, moving to a middle school in grade 6 lowered achievement by about 0.1–0.2 SD. The losses persisted and were larger for low achievers.
   - Florida found no high-school payoff. North Carolina found more discipline problems lasting into grade 9.
4. **Savings are real but modest, and usually below projections.**
   - Realized savings were "well under $1M per school" a year in six cities. DC's 2008 closures cost $39.5M against $9.7M first reported. Seattle's projection fell from $31.5M to about $5M before it withdrew its plan.
   - The largest savings came from cutting staff, not from buildings. Building sales rarely bring windfalls.
5. **Closures fall disproportionately on Black and low-income communities.** This holds even after accounting for enrollment and achievement.
   - Utilization formulas can carry forward past displacement. A Portland study links inner-Northeast under-enrollment to school choice plus gentrification.
   - In Chicago, utilization formulas steered closures toward schools with more special-education students.
6. **Boundary changes alone cause little disruption.** Wake County, NC found little or none, sometimes small gains.
7. **Vacant buildings are a long-term problem.**
   - In 12 cities, 301 closed buildings were unsold, and over 40% of reused ones went to charter schools.
   - A 2026 Chicago study links long-vacant closed schools to about 10% more shootings nearby; earlier crime studies are mixed.
8. **Process matters for legitimacy and outcomes.** Plans did better when districts:
   - made the case early and published clear, checkable criteria;
   - adjusted the list in response to evidence and voted once on a whole plan;
   - guaranteed displaced students seats in stronger schools;
   - avoided rushed timelines.

   Many districts (Seattle, San Francisco, Oakland, Chicago) paused or reversed course. Plans that passed (Denver, Pittsburgh) faced lawsuits or demands for cost detail.

## What this means for PPS (cautious)

**Supports parts of the plan**
- Enrollment decline is structural. Births fell about 20% after 2007, and Oregon had the largest share of "missing" students nationally.
- Consolidating small schools is roughly neutral for achievement in the medium run (Netherlands, Norway, Arkansas).
- Receiving-school harm can be avoided when newcomers are spread out and supported (Pittsburgh, Sweden).

**Raises questions about the plan**
- With non-failing schools closing, the main documented upside (a move to a clearly better school) is limited, while the disruption cost remains.
- The seven K-8 → K-5 conversions run against the strongest grade-configuration evidence.
- Minimum-size floors aren't research-based, and the large receiving schools some scenarios create sit above the sizes reviews recommend for disadvantaged students.
- Savings estimates elsewhere ran well above actual results. The November fiscal impact statement should:
    - show the realistic final list;
    - separate staffing savings from facility savings;
    - itemize one-time and transportation costs;
    - net out students who leave the district.
- Equity risks are well documented. An equity impact analysis, seat guarantees and published demographic data for closing and receiving schools are standard recommendations.
- Boundary changes alone are low-disruption, which gives some support to the "boundaries first" position.
- Closed-building plans matter. Vacancy is costly and charter reuse is common unless prohibited.

**For Sunnyside specifically** (see [`SUNNYSIDE_CASE.md`](SUNNYSIDE_CASE.md))
- **Strongest support:** the K-8 evidence. Keeping a full, working K-8 avoids a documented grade-6 transition loss.
- **Helpful, but don't overclaim:** outdoor learning's engagement, behavior and well-being benefits, especially for students with disabilities. It is not proven to raise test scores.
- **A counterargument to prepare for:** the magnet-segregation evidence. Sunnyside's actual draw and demographics (19% direct certification, 23.5% special education) are the answer.

## How board members' statements line up with the research

Directors' public remarks (Jan 2025 – Oct 2026), checked against the studies above. This reflects **what they said in meetings**, not everything they may know. Some attributions are "probable" because captions don't name speakers; see `board-members/<name>/*_transcript_excerpts_*.txt`.

**Overall.**
- **The board's case for consolidation rests on resource logic.** Fewer, fuller schools can each afford a librarian, special-education teacher, arts and electives. That is plausible, but the closure literature hasn't tested it as a route to better student outcomes.
- **Core findings rarely come up.** Few directors have raised what the research says matters most: that outcomes depend on the quality of the receiving school, that disruption starts at announcement, and that savings usually come in below projections. Engelsman and Sullivan come closest.
- **On K-8s, the record is split.** Wang cites unpublished 2017-era district data that conflicts with the strongest published studies; La Forte's updated view matches those studies.

**Who engages with the research most** (based on public remarks):
- **Engelsman** draws most explicitly and accurately on the closure literature (item 18), and caught that the 360 threshold comes with a class-size assumption (item 17). Some of her causal claims rest on anecdote (item 19).
- **La Forte** engages directly with research and changes her view when evidence warrants. Her one survey inference was overstated.
- **Chase-Miller** is strong on the equity, spatial and privatization literature, and weaker on K-8 outcome evidence.
- **Splitt** has strong fiscal and policy fluency (levy caps, Measures 5 and 50, staffing math) and repeatedly asks for data. Her K-8 views lean on anecdote.
- **Sullivan's** instincts mostly match the evidence (K-8s, savings), but she seldom cites research.
- **Wang** draws mainly on the Southeast Guiding Coalition experience and district data. His central K-8 claim rests on unpublished 2017-era district data that conflicts with the strongest published studies. His class-size and timeline claims are overstated.
- **DePass** frames things as systems thinking and is candid that savings aren't the goal. She cites research rarely, and made one demographic stretch.
- **Ian Ritorto**, the 2025-26 student representative, raised a sound methodological point about convenience-sampling bias in the district survey (Mar 31).

## Caveats

- **Paywalled papers** are summarized from abstracts and authors' public summaries. Open-access full text is saved where available.
- **Figures to check against the original before quoting:** Rockoff & Lockwood and Schwerdt & West effect sizes (from the authors' summaries); the Andrews et al. and Cotton size ranges; the Serbulo figures.
- **Non-peer-reviewed items** (reports, audits, news for 2022–2026 district events) are labeled in every index.
- **Topic C** includes recent district cases drawn from news coverage. Use them as context, not as evidence of effects.
